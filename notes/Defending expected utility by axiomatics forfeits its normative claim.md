# Defending expected utility by axiomatics forfeits its normative claim

Sections 1 and 2 of [[Doctor Wakker and Wang 2020 on the ergodicity problem]] are correct and self-defeating in the same movement.

**What is correct.** Expected utility is a representation theorem. Given a preference relation over probability distributions satisfying the axioms, there exists a utility function such that the relation is represented by $\sum_j p_j U(x_j)$. Ramsey, von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage require no ensemble, no repetition, no averaging over persons or time. Peters's "multiverse clones" is rhetoric, it invites this reply, and the reply is sound: nothing in the theorem asserts that the EU number is realised or consumed. The authors are also right that the isomorphism between aggregating over time, over people and over states is long known and unremarkable.

**What it costs.** A representation theorem makes no recommendation. If EU is only the statement that preferences satisfying certain axioms admit a certain functional form, then it cannot conflict with a growth-rate criterion, because it does not say what anyone *should* do — the utility function is whatever the agent's preferences make it. On that reading the two frameworks are not rivals and the Matters Arising has no target. But that reading also strips EU of everything that made it worth defending: the explanatory work it does across economics, where declining a favourable gamble is *explained* by concave utility, and concavity is *attributed* to psychology.

That explanatory use is what the ergodicity argument is about, and §1 and §2 never reach it. The claim is not that EU secretly contains an ergodic assumption in its axioms. It is that where EU is used to explain behaviour under repeated multiplicative exposure, **a taste has been posited where a dynamic would suffice** — and that the transformation which makes the observable ergodic is *fixed by the process* rather than chosen by the modeller. See [[Jensen supplies the gap but only the dynamic privileges the logarithm]] for why that is the whole of the framework's claim to non-arbitrariness, and for the live objection that specifying a dynamic is as free as specifying a utility function.

**The isomorphism concedes rather than settles.** If the mathematics of aggregating over time, over people and over states is the same mathematics, then the formalism cannot tell you which aggregation your problem calls for; only the interpretation can. Pointing at the isomorphism therefore establishes that interpretation is doing all the work — which is the ergodicity programme's thesis, not its refutation. The authors write "not EU, but Peters' theory needs an ergodicity assumption," and this is true of EU-as-axiomatised and false of EU-as-used, since dynamic economics iterates EU with an expectation taken at every date. Where that assumption is installed in models that do iterate is the subject of [[The representative agent is the ergodicity assumption in costume]].

**The narrow form of the reply.** Do not claim EU is internally incoherent; it is not. Claim that the axiomatic defence and the explanatory use cannot both be maintained — that a framework which disclaims normative content in section 1 may not, in section 3, produce a counterexample about what "virtually everyone will prefer." Preference-talk is normative talk, and it is smuggled back in three pages after being disowned. See [[The Wakker counterexample refutes expected log utility too]] for what that counterexample does when its own arithmetic is run out.
