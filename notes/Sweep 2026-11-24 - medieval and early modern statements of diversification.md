## New passages, by tradition

### Latin Christendom

**Benedetto Cotrugli, *Libro de l'arte de la mercatura* (Naples, 1458), I.xiiii, "De le sicurtà et sicuratori".** Checked: Ribaudo ed. 2016, p. 87 (DOI 10.14277/978-88-6969-088-4, open access).

> Deve assicurare al continuo et sopra ogni legnio, perché l'uno ristora l'altro.
>
> He should underwrite continually and on every hull, because the one makes good the other.

The only statement found anywhere in the sweep that names the *mechanism* rather than the rule: offsetting across hulls. It is the underwriter's maxim, the absorber diversifying across bottoms, which is [[Diversification runs across bottoms not across partners]] stated from the other side of the contract. Cevolini 2020 (*Soziale Systeme*, DOI 10.1515/sosys-2020-0024) reads it as the earliest formulation of the spread-of-risks principle of modern insurance; his English quotation adds a clause about pooling that is not in the sentence above and must come from the continuation [verify against p. 87 f.].

**Datini insurance policies (Prato, Archivio di Stato, Datini), 1389–1393.** Practice written into the clause, not a maxim. Ceccarelli 2007 (*JEHPS* 3:1, p. 9) reports a Florentine policy of 30 September 1393 on Spanish wool that was "to be divided and shipped on two different crafts" (AsPo 1159, doc. 35), several further policies covering one consignment across several vessels (p. 8 n. 24: AsPo 1158, docs. 33, 85, 122, 148; AsPo 1159, docs. 42, 50, 56, 64), and a Pisan policy of 1389 in which each underwriter took one good only (p. 13; AsPo 1158, doc. 68). He calls the whole "a flexible strategy of risk diversification" (p. 21, citing de Roover 1965, 70–105, for the habit of shipping small lots on many vessels). [verify against the policies.] For the coding: a policy that names several vessels is deed-level evidence of across-bottoms spreading.

**Giovanni di Pagolo Morelli, *Ricordi* (Florence, 1393–1421).** The counter-maxim, five centuries before Twain. In Bec's account (1967, ch. I), Morelli tells his heirs to trade alone, with their own money and never on borrowed money, and to make fewer trades provided they are safe. He also tells them to visit foreign factors in person at least once a year. That is concentration with monitoring, the "watch that basket" pole, not spreading. Bec gives French translations only; the Italian is to be pulled from Branca's edition (1956) [verify].

**Cervantes, *Don Quijote* I.23, and the egg-basket proverb.** This resolves one of the note's open flags. Sancho's line in Spanish:

> de sabios es guardarse hoy para mañana, y no aventurarse todo en un día.

There are no eggs. They enter with Motteux's English translation (1700; his Book III, ch. ix): "not to venture all his eggs in one basket" [verify against the 1700 print]. Earlier still, Torriano's *Second Alphabet* of Italian proverbial phrases (London, 1666; Wing T1928) is reported to gloss the egg phrase by the ship one: "To put all ones Eggs in a Paniard, viz., to hazard all in one bottom" [verify in EEBO, A62968]. If that holds, the *bottom* proverb, Antonio's idiom, is the older English form and the eggs are the later gloss.

### Jewish tradition

**Genesis 32:8–9 (AV 32:7–8), with Rashi.** Jacob divides people, flocks and camels "into two camps", reasoning that "if Esau come to the one company, and smite it, then the other company which is left shall escape" (AV). This is ruin avoidance by partition, and it works only if Esau cannot strike both, which is the independence condition engineered spatially. Rashi, following Tanḥuma, makes the division one of three preparations (gift, prayer, war); Ramban and Rashbam read the "war" as flight to save a part. One medieval commentator glosses that the camps stood far apart so that a disaster at one would be seen in time by the other [commentator to identify; the attribution online is confused]. Biblical rather than medieval, but the commentary is medieval and the reading of the division as survival of a part is theirs.

### Persia and the Islamic world

**Kaykāvus b. Eskandar, *Qābus-nāma* (1082), chapter on amassing wealth.** Checked in the Persian (Wikisource scan of a printed edition, at the page marked ص ۹۴) [verify edition and chapter number; Levy's translation, 1951, is in the workspace as Qab51]. My translation:

> Strive so that of every dirham you spend two *dāng* on your household and dependents, and however much you need it use no more than that. When those two *dāng* are gone, put two more *dāng* in store and do not think of them; leave them for your heirs and for the days of weakness and old age, so that they may come to your aid. Spend the two *dāng* that remain on your own *tajammul*, and let it be of things that do not die or wear out: jewels, gold, silver, brass, bronze and the like. If there is more than this, give it to the earth, for whatever you give to the earth you get back from it, the capital stays in place for ever, and the profit flows and is lawful.

He goes on to forbid selling the household's durables in a pinch in the hope of buying them back. A division by fraction with a reserve tranche, the same architecture as Bava Metzia 42a's thirds, and the surplus goes into land. Where the Talmud's third "in hand" is liquidity, Kaykāvus's reserve is set aside for old age and heirs and is not to be touched.

**al-Dimashqī, *Kitāb al-Ishāra ilā maḥāsin al-tijāra* (before 570/1175).** Not a diversification text on what was read. A secondary account says he tells the merchant to spend a little less than he earns, so as to hold a reserve against loss (Setia 2011, *Islam & Science* 9:1) [verify; not read]. The chapter in Tad95 gives his fourfold classification of property (*al-māl al-ṣāmit*, *al-ʿarḍ*, *al-ʿaqqār*, *al-ḥayawān*; p. 220) but no rule for spreading across the four. Ritter's German translation (*Der Islam* 7, 1917) is the place to look.

**Ibn al-Jaṣṣāṣ and the wazir Ibn al-Furāt, in al-Tanūkhī, *Nishwār al-muḥāḍara* II, 36–39 (parallel: Ibn al-Jawzī, *Akhbār al-ḥamqā*, 44–47).** A council on where to keep wealth: commercial property, agricultural land, silver coin, or gems, the last because they are light, easily concealed and readily turned into cash (Marmer 2026, *Journal of Abbasid Studies*, p. 92; DOI 10.1163/22142371-00802038). The PDF that was reached ends before the jeweller's own answer. A lead, not yet a finding; the setting is confiscation risk, a shock that strikes every asset the confiscator can see, so concealability is a decorrelation device.

**Sa'di.** Parnian and Hosseini Abbariki 2013 survey economic advice in the *Būstān* and *Gulistān*; not read.

### South Asia

**Sigālovāda Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya 31), verse at the close of the householder's section.** Checked in Pali (Ānandajoti's text):

> Ekena bhoge bhuñjeyya, dvīhi kammaṁ payojaye,
> catutthañ ca nidhāpeyya, āpadāsu bhavissati.
>
> With one part let him enjoy his wealth, with two let him carry on his work, and the fourth let him lay by: it will be there in misfortunes.

Canonical rather than medieval, but transmitted with Buddhaghosa's fifth-century commentary, which calls the sutta the householder's *vinaya*. It is the third fractional rule with a reserve tranche: Bava Metzia, the *Qābus-nāma* and the Sigālovāda all set the reserve at a fixed share, a third or a quarter.

**Kauṭilya.** Sihag 2008 argues for an explicit diversification principle in the *Arthaśāstra*; not read and no passage identified. Treat it as a claim until the passage is in hand.

**Geniza.** The note's flag on Goitein stands. What the sweep confirms is a different practice. India-trade letters were sent in duplicate or triplicate on separate ships (Goitein and Friedman, *India Traders*, ch. 1, DOI 10.1163/EJ.9789004154728.I-918.6, as summarised by the search; the PDF that was reached is a two-page excerpt; the registry dates the chapter 2007, the book is 2008 [verify]). That spreads *information* across hulls, not goods. Gil 2008 and Goitein's *Mediterranean Society* I remain the places to check for split consignments.

### East Asia

**Shimai Sōshitsu, testament in seventeen articles to his heir Tokuzaemon Nobuyoshi (Hakata, 1610).** This bears directly on the note's Japanese hypothesis. Sorokina and Shashkov 2019 (*Manuscript* 12:8, p. 72; DOI 10.30853/manuscript.2019.8.13) report two prescriptions. The first is to put savings into several businesses so that a failure does not take everything. The second is not to commit large sums to fitting out ships for China and the rest of Asia, and to turn to the domestic market instead. Both are secondary paraphrases, made from a Russian online translation, with no article numbers [verify against the Japanese text; the full English translation is in Vaporis 2018, and Ramseyer 1979 discusses the code]. If the Japanese bears them out, the earliest surviving merchant house code states *both* terms of the ordering. It spreads capital across businesses and it avoids the ruin-prone venture outright. The note's suggestion that the Edo merchant world wrote down only the avoidance term would then hold, at most, for the later codes.

**Practice without maxim.** Oka and Gipouloux 2013 (*Itinerario* 37:3; DOI 10.1017/S0165115313000831) on Hakata and Sakai investors pooling capital in Ming-licensed and Nagasaki voyages through *nagegane*, a shipwreck-exempt investment akin to bottomry. Mitsui reserves for bad debts and repairs (Nisikawa 1984, *Japan Business History Review* 19:3, on the Ryōgae-dana ichimaki accounts, 1729–1870). The Ming and Qing *shangshu* counsel caution and prudence (Qiu 2015 on Wu Zhongfu's *Shanggu bianlan*; Clydesdale 2017 on the *Shanggu xingmi*, 1635), but no spreading passage was identified. Chen, Yun and Lin 2019 reconstruct elite Qing asset portfolios from confiscation archives. For Korea, Yi Chae-un's *Haedong hwasik chŏn* (c. 1750; Ahn 2019) is a wealth-management text to read.

## What this changes in the proverbs note

1. **The mechanism is stated once, by an insurer.** The note's claim that no saying shows anyone computing the gain still stands. But Cotrugli names *why* spreading works, offsetting, and does so as an underwriter writing across many hulls. Antonio states the rule; Cotrugli states the reason.
2. **A recurring structure: the fractional rule with a reserve tranche.** Bava Metzia (thirds), the *Qābus-nāma* (sixths in three pairs, surplus to land) and the Sigālovāda (quarters) each prescribe fixed shares, and each fences one share off as reserve. The reserve is distance from the barrier, held by rule rather than by judgement. Whether the Persian and Jewish rules are connected is open: both sit in Sasanian and post-Sasanian Iraq and Iran, so transmission cannot be excluded on the face of it (cf. [[Transmission versus independent recurrence is the load-bearing test]]).
3. **The egg-basket attribution is settled in outline.** It is not Cervantes; it is Motteux in 1700, and Torriano in 1666 if the EEBO check holds. Both subject to the two flags above.
4. **The counter-maxim is medieval.** Morelli's "fewer trades, but safe ones", made alone and with one's own money, is the Twain position in 1400s Florence. It should sort with controllable peril and close monitoring, as the bottoms note predicts.
5. **The Japanese hypothesis is weakened, not refuted**, pending the Shimai text.
6. **The silence on correlation persists.** No passage found warns that spreading across ventures struck by one storm buys nothing. Jacob's two camps come nearest: they work only if the attacker cannot reach both, and one commentator noticed that this required distance.
