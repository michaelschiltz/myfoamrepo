# The hexagon is optimal only among neighbours

**The regular hexagon is not the best shape for a cell. It is the best shape for a cell among cells. Standing alone, a unit-area cell should be round; inside a comb whose walls are shared, the hexagon is optimal, and every cell in it pays less wax than any solitary cell could. The optimum is defined on the configuration and enjoyed by each member severally. That is *Meditations* 6.54 in geometry. The walls are cost-sharing, not risk-pooling; but what the walls hold is the colony's store, which is a risk pool. The hexagon is the cheap container of a pool — and that, not the geometry, is what the image may be asked to carry.**

## The arithmetic

Wall per unit-area cell:

| Configuration | Wall charged to one cell |
| --- | --- |
| Solitary circle | $2\sqrt{\pi} \approx 3.545$ |
| Solitary regular hexagon | $2\sqrt[4]{12} \approx 3.722$ |
| Solitary square | $4$ |
| Square tiling, walls shared | $2$ |
| Hexagonal tiling, walls shared | $\sqrt[4]{12} \approx 1.861$ |

Taken alone the hexagon loses to the circle. Circles cannot tile, so a comb of circles wastes wax in the interstices and shares no wall. The hexagon wins only because sharing halves its cost, and among the tilings it halves best: about 7 per cent less wall than the square tiling, 18 per cent less than the triangular. **No cell's own geometry recommends the hexagon. Only the comb does.**

## The proof has the same anatomy

Hales's argument (see [[Hales 2001 - the honeycomb theorem and the architecture of its proof]]) bounds each cell separately, and the per-cell bound is useless on its own: it carries a bulge term and a side-count term of either sign. Both vanish only in the sum, and for two reasons that belong to the whole and to no part. A wall bulging out of one cell bulges into its neighbour by the same area, so the bulge terms cancel in pairs across shared walls. And the torus's Euler characteristic forces the mean number of sides to six, so the side-count terms cancel in aggregate. **The individual inequality becomes informative only through the shared boundary and the global constraint.**

## The epigraph reading

*Meditations* 6.54 — τὸ τῷ σμήνει μὴ συμφέρον οὐδὲ τῇ μελίσσῃ συμφέρει [verify] — is retained because it states the alignment of collective and individual advantage directly ([[Stoic loci on cooperation - only two argue from interdependence]]). The comb is the material object behind the image, and it shows the alignment without altruism: no cell sacrifices for the comb; each is cheaper in it. That is the structure of the pooling proposition — the case for cooperation never passes through obligation — and it is cognate with Seneca's arch (*Ep.* 95.53), which also stands by mutual pressure, more than with the membership loci.

## What the image can and cannot carry

The geometry is additive and static. Wall lengths sum; nothing compounds; there is no time, no trajectory, no absorbing barrier. The walls' economy is the economy of a **shared fixed cost** — one wall serving two cells, as one escort serves a convoy or one warehouse a guild. Taken alone, the comb is not a figure of the time-average mechanism, which raises the long-run growth rate by averaging fluctuations under multiplicative dynamics ([[Time-average optimization is a survivorship property not an intention]], [[Pooling's sign is set by the correlation of its baskets]]).

*Corrected 4 November 2026.* The first version stopped there and filed the comb as an emblem of cost-sharing only. That is too narrow, because it ignores what the comb is *for*. The cells hold the colony's stores, and the stores are a risk pool in the strict sense: honey gathered by many foragers under fluctuating conditions, held in common and distributed through dearth and winter. **The comb is the infrastructure of the pool, and shared walls lower the price of the storage that collective buffering requires.** Cost-sharing and risk-pooling are not alternatives here but two layers of one arrangement: the fixed-cost economy makes the variance-pooling affordable. The proper historical analogues are therefore the institutions that combine the two — the guild granary, the common warehouse of a merchant nation, the fund of a *kō* held between draws — rather than deterministic cost-sharing alone. *General average* remains outside the image: it apportions a stochastic realised loss and carries its own correlation structure.

Two limits remain. The image still has no barrier and no correlation structure in its geometry; it shows the container, not the dynamics of what is contained. And the contrast with stingless bees shows that sociality alone does not produce the shared-wall form: meliponine colonies are highly eusocial and perennial, yet store honey in clusters of oval cerumen pots that are dismantled when empty and their material reused. The honey bee economises on wax by sharing walls in durable infrastructure; the stingless bee by recycling material in structures it builds and pulls down. Read as analogy only, these are the standing fund and the pool that dissolves and re-forms ([[Pooling approaches the infinite ensemble and perpetual succession the infinite horizon]]). Darwin's use of the same bees as a graded series is treated at [[Darwin's bee series is a space-for-time substitution that licenses possibility not pathway]].
