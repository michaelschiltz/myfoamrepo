# Distinguish strong from weak ergodicity breaking

The physics literature on disordered systems and anomalous diffusion long ago split the phenomenon in two, and the vocabulary should be imported wholesale rather than reinvented.

**Strong breaking** is decomposition of the state space: invariant sets exist, and no amount of waiting connects them. This is the absorbing case, and it is ergodicity failure in the strict measure-theoretic sense — see [[An absorbing barrier breaks ergodicity by construction]].

**Weak breaking** is the harder and more interesting case. No decomposition occurs; the state space remains connected in principle. But characteristic timescales diverge — waiting times drawn from heavy-tailed distributions, ageing, sub-diffusion — so that finite-time averages fail to converge to ensemble values over any horizon the system actually occupies. The system is ergodic in the limit and non-ergodic in practice (Bouchaud 1992; Bel and Barkai 2005; Metzler et al. 2014).

The distinction earns its place because **our historical cases are mostly of the second kind, and we have been writing as though they were of the first.** A merchant house is not literally absorbed at every reversal; a trading régime is not formally irreducible. What is true is that the horizon over which the ensemble equivalence would be recovered vastly exceeds the horizon over which any historical agent, firm or polity operates. That is weak breaking, and saying so is both more accurate and more defensible than asserting decomposition where none exists. It also supplies the answer to the objection that institutions plainly *did* recover from reversals: recovery is compatible with weak breaking and fatal only to a careless strong claim.

Using "non-ergodic" unqualified is therefore exactly where a referee will locate slippage, because the unqualified word lets us claim the rhetorical force of decomposition while the evidence supports only timescale separation. Qualify it every time. This is the same discipline the vault already imposes on `convergence` versus `parallelism` and on the two senses of lock-in.
