# Reed and Bekar 2003 on the usury ban as protection of the survival pool

Clyde G. Reed and Cliff T. Bekar, "Religious Prohibitions against Usury," *Explorations in Economic History* 40 (2003), 347–368. DOI 10.1016/S0014-4983(03)00039-1. Received 29 August 2001. Read in full (October 2026). Precursor: Cliff Bekar, "Income Sharing Amongst Medieval Peasants: Usury Prohibitions and the Non-Market Provision of Insurance," Lewis and Clark College working paper, 2001.

**The closest neighbour of the project in the whole sweep. The model puts the absorbing barrier at its centre. Risk-neutral agents mean that all the value of pooling comes from the barrier, and the Church's preferences rank survival above everything else. Where it stops short is debt: credit never carries anyone to the barrier. The danger of the capital market is to the pool, not to the borrower.**

## The argument

- **The claim (p. 348).** The prohibitions were a "negative 'tie-in' between salvation and consumption lending at interest". The Church used them to defend charity and informal pooling, the two devices that kept the poor alive, against competition from the capital market.
- **The target is the existence of the market, not its price (p. 348).** The authors read the Church's rhetoric as aiming to eliminate the capital market, not to lower interest rates. They note (n. 2) that the bans probably *raised* rates. They quote Noonan: the main economic result was to restrain Christians from small loans and "to stimulate a greater use of risk-sharing investments."
- **The chronology is the test (pp. 350–351).** Six phases run from 500 to 1830. Any explanation must account for:
  - why usury mattered little before the twelfth century;
  - why the ban peaked in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, at a zero rate and aimed at consumption loans;
  - why enforcement relaxed in the fifteenth century, with the *monti di pietà* charging sanctioned interest;
  - why it hardened again in the sixteenth;
  - why it ended in 1830.

## The model (pp. 357–360)

- **Agents.** Income is y_H or y_L with y_L < m < y_H, where m is subsistence. An agent below m starves. There are rich and poor types with different probabilities of the good state. All agents are risk-neutral and face independent shocks (p. 357).
- **The Church.** Its preferences are lexicographic: "Its foremost goal is to keep all members of the population alive," because that raises the value of Church lands and the number of souls saved (p. 357). It sells a guarantee of salvation for a fee x paid in good states, and uses the revenue to lift agents in bad states to m. Pooling and charity are collapsed into that one transfer.
- **The value function.** An agent who stays out of the scheme starves in every bad state. Its value is a geometric sum over its remaining life, in which the per-period chance of staying alive (the probability of the good state times the probability of not dying from other causes) multiplies across periods. Joining the scheme removes starvation from the path (pp. 357–358).
- **The capital market (p. 359).**
  - Lenders are risk-neutral, information is complete, and loans "leave the present value of the lender's net wealth unchanged".
  - A rich borrower takes a new loan of (m − y_L) in each bad state and repays nothing until his debt reaches D_max, the expected present value of his future income. He is thus "assured of never starving."
  - D_max is negative for the poor, so they cannot borrow.
  - Debt "accumulates deterministically over time".
- **The mechanism (pp. 359–360).** The capital market raises the rich agent's outside option. That lowers the fee the Church can charge him, so the pool can no longer cover its payouts. The ban lowers the probability of salvation k for those who borrow at interest, to k_u = 0 "for a true believer". That restores the rich agent's participation and the pool's revenue.

## The comparative statics, and why they matter here (pp. 362–365)

- **Variance drives the ban.** "Falling mean income and a constant or increasing variability of income implies an increasing probability of starvation," so the Church should intensify the ban (p. 362).
  - Their evidence runs through volatility. English grain prices "fluctuated by an average of 26.6% from one year to the next" in 1280–1350, but only 11.5% in 1440–1500 (p. 363, n. 51). The ban peaked in the first period and relaxed in the second.
  - Note 48 adds that rising variance may shift people into informal pooling of its own accord, as repayment risk rises.
- **Land is the buffer.** Landholding smooths consumption, so the ban tightens as the share of smallholders and the landless grows (pp. 362–363).
- **Table 1 (p. 363)** matches each period's land distribution, population and real-wage trends, and alternatives to the pool against the strength of the restrictions.
- **The end (p. 364).** The ban ended in 1830 because agricultural output rose with lower variance, the poor laws took over relief, and private insurance spread.
- **Zero, and consumption loans only (pp. 364–365).** A zero rate made the capital market least attractive and was the only rate defensible in natural law. Investment loans were spared because banning them would lower growth, and "in a dynamic model this could lead to depressed mean incomes" (p. 365). Merchant bankers were also the Church's allies. Their damnation was offset by charity.

## How close it comes to the time-average view

- **All the value is in the barrier.** Risk neutrality removes the concave-utility motive. Pooling is worth something only because it removes starvation from the path.
- **Survival compounds.** The value function is a sum over a path truncated by absorption, with survival probabilities multiplying across periods. The agent values its life path, not one period.
- **The institution is safety-first.** The Church's survival-first lexicographic preference is the institutional form of Scott's safety-first ([[Scott 1976 on the subsistence ethic as safety-first]]). The agents maximise expected value; the institution protects survival. That is the two-level structure of [[Taleb secures the collective by exit and has no theory of pooling]], with pooling instead of exit.
- **The empirical section is about sequences, not states (p. 355).** Every spell of "starvation" wages in 1300–1347 lasted longer than a year. In all but one, the loan "could not have been repaid before the next period of starvation wages began."
- **Volatility, not the mean alone, sets the ban (pp. 362–363).** In the vault's terms, the ban tracks the σ² term.
- **Aggregate shocks are handled (p. 353, n. 27).** With homogeneous agents, pooling smooths only idiosyncratic shocks. With aggregate shocks and heterogeneous agents, it "can still be effective in increasing community survivability by transferring income from those above subsistence to those below." That bears directly on [[Pooling's sign is set by the correlation of its baskets]]: under correlated shocks, survival needs transfers from the rich, not a pool of equals.

## Where it stops short

- **Debt is harmless in the model.** The lender is evaluated by expected present value, and the borrower is kept alive by contract. The fixed claim never pushes anyone towards the barrier. In the project's reading, and in Rubin's ([[Rubin 2009 on the interest ban as a curb on overborrowing under Church insurance]]), debt is itself a route to absorption.
- **The model contradicts its own evidence.** The shocks are independent from period to period, while p. 355 documents persistent ones. With persistence, debt accumulated to D_max during a run of bad years becomes absorbing. That is the step the project can take.
- **No wealth, no growth rate.** Income is additive and nothing accumulates except debt. The σ² effect enters through the probability of starvation, not through a growth rate.
- **The agent's criterion is still an expectation.** Agents maximise expected discounted value with an exogenous discount factor. The trajectory enters through survival weights, not through a time-average criterion.
- **The ban works through belief.** It acts by lowering the probability of salvation for borrowers, not by changing the dynamics of debt.

## Relation to the neighbours

- **Against Ekelund, Hébert and Tollison (p. 348).** The Church was on average a net lender. The ban was also enforced on the clergy, which a monopoly explanation cannot account for.
- **Against Posner 1995 (p. 349).** In Posner's account, a welfare floor tempts the poor into risky borrowing, and usury limits ration their credit. Reed and Bekar object that medieval poverty came from shocks — "illness, accidents, premature death of bread-winners … bad weather" (quoting Dyer) — not from risky investment, and that Posner cannot explain a zero rate. Rubin 2009 rebuilds Posner's mechanism for consumption borrowing and acknowledges the debt (Rubin n. 4). The disagreement between Reed–Bekar and Rubin is the old one over whether the moral-hazard side of insurance matters.
- **Glaeser and Scheinkman.** Same consumption/investment split ([[Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998 on interest restrictions as social insurance]]), different mechanism: protecting a survival pool rather than an ex-ante transfer between states.
- **Hudson.** The same split in Mesopotamia, between barley debts and commercial debts ([[Hudson 2002 on clean slates as protection of the free population]]).

## Use

Cite this paper as the published statement that the usury ban protected survival, before any claim of novelty. The project's contribution is the step the model assumes away: debt dynamics under persistent or multiplicative shocks, with the fixed claim as the route to absorption. The test is longitudinal absorption among households holding fixed claims. Their chronology (Table 1) and the grain-price volatility series are a ready-made first check of whether the ban's intensity tracked σ².
