# The fictive household supplied partition and perpetuity without legal personality

D'Amico's claim is that Tokugawa merchants exploited an underappreciated feature of the *ie*: **its name did not need to correspond to a real person**. On that fact they built fictive households which on paper looked like members of the founder's lineage, but were founded in partnership with unrelated merchants, staffed by non-kin professional managers, and whose membership need not overlap with the actual family at all. Fictive households could in turn become founding partners in further fictive households, mushrooming across the archipelago. He names this the *fictive household partnership model*, against the *lineage group* (*dōzokudan*) model the literature has assumed.

The components arrive without the legal machinery the comparative literature treats as their precondition. Shares are real and denominated: the house Nishikawa Denji was formed between 1741 and 1757 by twenty-one investors holding forty shares to trade dried sea slug from Matsumae to Nagasaki; the Nakai's Sendai store took four passive partners at 500 *ryō* apiece and 312.5 *ryō* apiece, their *mōshōkin* entitling them to interest and dividends, with *kuchi* allocated in proportion and profit and loss split accordingly. Perpetuity is real: the household persisted irrespective of whose body occupied its name, and could contract. Asset partition is real and was the point — Nakai Shinsaburō in Sendai, being a separate household from Nakai Genzaemon in Hino, bore no responsibility for the latter's debts, and the lord of Sendai could not force donations or extract service from Genzaemon.

**The partition is jurisdictional rather than juridical, and that is the finding.** Assets were divided among entities scattered across a landscape split between shogunal territory, the suzerain's, and some 260 domains, exploiting the cost of collecting a debt across a border. Shielding was achieved by drafting a name and choosing a jurisdiction, not by obtaining a grant. This is entity shielding produced entirely by instruments — precisely the sense in which institutions are transmitted as texts and drafting practices ([[Never let the units slide from instruments to populations]]).

What makes it a counter-case rather than an analogue is the legal environment. The samurai authorities enforced private contracts and distinguished *kane kuji* from *hon kuji*; Osaka's town governor built a framework favouring creditors on non-collateralised loans, which Edo adopted only in 1843. But they **flatly refused to adjudicate partnership accounts**, designating them *nakama-goto*, in-group matters, and therefore non-adjudicable. The law offered no tool for forming a business and no help to a creditor collecting after a partnership dissolved. So the components did not merely arrive without enabling law; they arrived against a standing refusal. That refusal is evidence about the filter, not about the merchants ([[Refusals are observations of the filter not inferences from survivors]]).

The consequence for the five-component framework is direct and uncomfortable for the received account. Entity shielding, perpetual succession and transferable claims are all present; legal personality in the juridical sense is absent, and the entities functioned anyway. Whether the framework should treat legal personality as one component among five or as a *substitutable* one is now a live question rather than a definitional matter — and the Japanese case answers it from outside the Roman-law lineage entirely, which is what makes it worth more than another European variant ([[Harris 2020 on the late plurality of limited liability]], [[The corporation is not the omega - the naties are an undisplaced niche]]).

One lexical observation worth pursuing rather than asserting. The unit of participation in these partnerships is 口 (*kuchi*) — the same word counting shares in the *tanomoshi-kō* 仕法帳, where 口数 fixes the membership and 壱人前 the contribution per unit. Whether that is one instrument-vocabulary spanning pooling and partnership, or homonymy, is a question for the documents.
