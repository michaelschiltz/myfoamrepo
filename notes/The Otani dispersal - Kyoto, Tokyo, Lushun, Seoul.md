# The Ōtani dispersal — Kyoto, Tokyo, Lüshun, Seoul

**Three expeditions between 16 August 1902 and 10 July 1914, led by Count Ōtani Kōzui (1876–1948), twenty-second abbot of Nishi Honganji** — the only major Silk Road expeditions mounted by **a religious institution rather than a state**. The third, 1910–14, bought **369 manuscript scrolls from Wang Yuanlu for 400 taels** at Dunhuang.

**Ōtani resigned in 1914 and the collection scattered.** Where it went:

- **Ryukoku University, Kyoto** — about **9,000 items**: [[Ryukoku University and the Otani collection]].
- **Lüshun Museum, Dalian** — **16,035 Buddhist manuscript fragments**, the largest single share, left in Japanese-held Manchuria.
- **National Library of China** — **621 Dunhuang scrolls moved from Lüshun**.
- **National Museum of Korea** — **373 objects given to the Governor-General in 1916** by the businessman Kuhara Fusanosuke, who had bought Ōtani's villa; the Central Asian collection there now numbers **more than 1,500 objects**, of which about **150** are permanently displayed since 2005. **Damaged in the Korean War** — including a Loulan mummy — **evacuated to Busan in three truckloads in 1951 and returned to Seoul in 1974.**
- **Tokyo National Museum** — the remaining Ōtani items **purchased in 1964 and moved in 1967**.

## What it does to the evidence

**This is successor custody with no successor.** A private collection whose owner's finances failed **followed the money and the empire**: to a university of his own sect, to a museum in a Japanese-held port, to a colonial governor-general, to a national museum of a state that did not yet exist. **Every subsequent boundary change cut the collection again.**

**And it is the survey's sharpest instance of the counting problem**: 16,035 fragments in Dalian, 621 scrolls in Beijing, 9,000 items in Kyoto, 1,500 objects in Seoul — **four institutions, four counting units, one expedition.**
