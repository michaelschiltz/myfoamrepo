# Social immunity is a consequence of individuation not evidence for it

**The colony that fights disease as a unit is usually cited as proof that the hive is more than the sum of its bees. The founding literature runs the inference the other way. Social immunity in the strict sense exists only where the unit of selection has already shifted from the worker to the colony through a germline–soma division (Cremer, Pull and Fürst 2018, 107–8). The immune function presupposes the new individual; it does not demonstrate it. What it adds is the apparatus by which an individual, once constituted, draws and defends its own boundary.**

## The order of explanation

Families defend their young by parental care; communal breeders gain additive protection from aggregation. Neither has social immunity on Cremer's account, because in neither has selection moved to a reproductive unit above the member. Unconditional altruism in disease defence is predicted for obligately eusocial superorganisms and only context-dependent defence for facultatively social species, whose members still reproduce and so retain interests against the group. The premise is stated flatly in Cremer and Sixt 2009, 131: the colony, not any member, produces offspring. The analogy with an organism's immune system holds because there is now a reproductive unit to defend.

## What immunity adds: the boundary

Pull and McMahon 2020 treat colony immunity as itself a major transition in immune-system evolution, and list among its functions not only defence against pathogens but the policing of internal cheaters (worker-laid eggs removed) and the maintenance of colony identity by colony-specific odour. Immunity is the machinery of the *self*: against parasites outside, defectors inside, and fusion with other colonies. That is the image the hive can lend the project — not cooperation, which the comb already shows ([[The hexagon is optimal only among neighbours]]), but a cooperative unit that has become a bearer of interests and polices its own edge.

## What it must not be asked to carry

The colony–organism parallel is a functional homoplasy, declared phenomenological by its own authors (Cremer and Sixt 2009, 129), with stated disanalogies: workers are genetically diverse, colonies can expel members and abandon nests, and there is no social autoimmunity. The project's discipline is the same ([[Homoplasy is the finding not the noise]]). Nor does the literature establish that collective defence lets individual defence atrophy: the *Lasius* result is pathogen-specific and its erosion hypothesis untested, and the bees' thin immune repertoire precedes their sociality ([[Social immunity and superorganism immunity - loci]]). A transfer to institutions that has collective protection hollowing out individual protection must be stated as a possibility.

## Revision 2026-11-13

Two full-text readings bear on this note. **Support:** Boomsma and Gawne 2018 (19) state that caste-differentiated superorganisms, like the co-replicons of a multicellular body, "lack cross-purpose conflict" over "resource acquisition, infrastructure maintenance, and immune defence" — immune defence is one of the functions that becomes conflict-free only after the point of no return. **Qualification:** Pull and McMahon fold the policing of internal cheaters into superorganism immunity. Gardner and Grafen 2009 prove that policing cannot be a group adaptation, and Boomsma and Gawne date it after the transition, as a response to falling relatedness. Defence against parasites and maintenance of colony identity belong to the new individual. Policing is better read as the members' response to their own heterogeneity ([[Three immune functions mark a pool that has become a person]], revision of the same date).
