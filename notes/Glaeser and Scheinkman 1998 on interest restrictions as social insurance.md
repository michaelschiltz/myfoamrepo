# Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998 on interest restrictions as social insurance

Edward L. Glaeser and José Scheinkman, "Neither a Borrower nor a Lender Be: An Economic Analysis of Interest Restrictions and Usury Laws," *Journal of Law and Economics* 41:1 (1998), 1–36. DOI 10.1086/467383. NBER Working Paper 4954 (1994) is the open version.

**The anchor of the functionalist literature on usury, and the template the project has to distinguish itself from.**

**The argument** [verify against the text; read so far only through abstracts and the secondary literature]. Interest restrictions work as crude social insurance. Where borrowing is driven by negative income shocks, those who borrow are those with bad draws and those who lend are those with good ones. A cap on the rate therefore transfers from the lucky to the unlucky, and agents choosing before they know their draw can prefer the restriction. The account predicts restrictions where income variance is high and credit markets are thin, and their erosion as commerce turns borrowing from distress-driven into investment-driven. Its reach across the Hebrew Bible, Aristotle, Islam and the medieval Church is the paper's selling point.

**Why it matters.** It is the strongest published statement that an interest ban has a function, and that the function is protection of the borrower in the bad state. The project needs it and needs to depart from it.

**Where the project departs.** "Insurance" here is ex-ante expected utility: the rule is judged across an ensemble of agents and draws. Nothing in the argument asks whether the borrower's *trajectory* reaches a barrier. Restated in time-average terms, a distress loan at a high rate to a household near subsistence is a multiplicative loss in the bad state. The cap protects the household from being absorbed, and smoothing consumption is incidental. It is the same rule with a different explanandum. See [[Doctrine has been read as insurance and never as ruin avoidance]] and [[Economics had its ensemble moment in 1738 and psychologized it]].

**What tells against it.** Temin and Voth's natural experiment on the 1714 cut in England's legal interest-rate ceiling (*Economic Journal* 2008, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2008.02140.x) finds credit access worse for borrowers with little social capital and no insurance gain. That tests the welfare claim; it does not test a barrier claim.
