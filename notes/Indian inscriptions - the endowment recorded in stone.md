# Indian inscriptions — the endowment recorded in stone

**The oldest South Asian evidence for a funded institution is epigraphic, and unlike the Chinese guild stele it records the terms of the fund.**

## The instance that matters

**In the Nasik caves, Uṣavadāta — son-in-law of the Kṣaharāta ruler Nahapāna, early second century CE — made permanent endowments with two weavers' guilds (*śreṇi*) at Govardhana**: one of **2,000 kārṣāpaṇas**, one of **1,000**, the interest to provide cloth money and other maintenance for monks at the cave monastery, **the agreement proclaimed before and approved by the *nigama sabhā*, the town assembly**. The principal was to remain with the guild in perpetuity and only the interest to be spent. ⚠️ **The interest rates usually quoted — one per cent and three-quarters of one per cent per month — were not verified against Senart's edition in this pass, and the whole reading here is from secondary web accounts.**

**Read as an institution, this is a perpetual endowment whose trustee is a craft corporation and whose beneficiaries are third parties, registered with a public body.** It is the structure the survey has now met three times elsewhere: the waqf, the *heqdesh* at Fustat, the Chinese lineage estate. **Here the trustee is a guild rather than a family or a community — and the deposit earns a stated rate.**

**A second, later instance of guild permanence**: the **Mandasor (Daśapura) silk weavers' inscription**, recording a sun temple built by the guild in 436 CE and repaired by it in 473, after members had dispersed into other trades — **the corporation outliving the occupation that named it.** ⚠️ Dates as usually given; not verified from Fleet's edition.

## Where the texts are

- **Published**: *Epigraphia Indica* (from 1892), *South Indian Inscriptions*, the *Annual Report on Indian Epigraphy*. Old volumes are scanned on the Internet Archive. **Layer 3, free, and a century old.**
- **Unpublished, and most of it**: a 1973 estimate quoted by the Digital Archive of South Indian Inscriptions put **about 25,000 inscriptions in Tamil Nadu of which some 6,000 were published.** ⚠️ Estimate old and second-hand; treat as an order of magnitude. **The estampages are with the Archaeological Survey's epigraphy branch** — see [[ASI epigraphy and the published corpora]].
- **Encoded**: [[DHARMA - an ERC Synergy project that produced a TEI corpus of inscriptions]].

## What it does to the evidence

**1. The record type sets what can be coded — and here it is generous.** An endowment inscription states the donor, the principal, the trustee, the rate, the purpose and the witness. **That is `MC1`, `CN1`, `GV1`, `PY0` and `DR1` in one sentence of Prakrit.** What it does not state is whether the terms were kept, and for how long.

**2. The survival rule is publicity.** An endowment was inscribed *because* the donor wanted it permanent and public. **Funds that were neither — a merchant's private reserve, a household's grain store — left no stone.** So the epigraphic census over-counts the pious, the perpetual and the donated, and under-counts everything contributory. **Pattern 7 in its ancient form.**

**3. It is the cleanest layer-3 corpus in the non-European survey and the least used by economic historians.** ⚠️ That last claim is an impression from this pass, not a bibliometric fact.
