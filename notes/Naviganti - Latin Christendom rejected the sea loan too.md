# Naviganti - Latin Christendom rejected the sea loan too

The frame "why didn't the sea loan migrate into Islam?" is the wrong question, because *neither* monotheism would house it. Gregory IX's decretal **Naviganti** — *Decretales Gregorii IX* (Liber Extra), X 5.19.19, "Naviganti vel eunti ad nundinas," promulgated 1234 (**verify book/title/chapter against a canon-law source; the numbering transposes easily**) — condemned the maritime lender who takes interest for bearing risk as a usurer. The identical doctrinal squeeze.

So the real comparative question is why the *shared* rejection produced *divergent* workarounds. **Latin Europe unbundled** the composite: the pure loan split off as the *bill of exchange*, the pure risk-transfer crystallized as the free-standing *marine insurance* contract in fourteenth-century Genoa and Florence. The **Islamic world** met the same function with the PLS partnership and never needed to unbundle at all. Same doctrinal problem, two institutional solutions — the payoff Harris walks straight past.

## What the rejection does and does not date

Lohsse's reconstruction of the juristic discourse sharpens what the prohibition licenses as evidence. *Naviganti* did not merely forbid the sea loan's interest; because its prohibition reached interest promised **solely as consideration for assuming peril**, it caught the very thing a priced risk-transfer would have to be. Jurists at the start of the thirteenth century — the controversy Raymund de Peñafort reports — were already arguing for exactly that payment. They were *zunächst erstickt*, and prevailed only in the fourteenth century.

So the rejection is real and its date is firm, but it dates a **doctrinal permission**, not the arrival of a conception. Reading the appearance of priced instruments around 1300 as the moment a peril became thinkable as a distribution mistakes the lifting of a prohibition for a change of mind.
