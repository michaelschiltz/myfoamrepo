# Read the Nakai anomaly against the ie's horizon not the transaction's

Katō leaves the 中井家 valuation anomaly open — some other logic underlies the house's reckoning, and he declines to name it ([[The Nakai ledger scores as losses what present value scores as gains]]). The hypothesis worth testing is that the accounting unit is the *ie* rather than the transaction, and that the horizon is accordingly the house's trajectory rather than the venture's return.

On that reading the asymmetry stops being an error. Leaving 掛金 unpriced while pricing 落札金 is exactly what a house does when contributions are not an investment seeking a return but the cost of remaining a member of a standing network — one whose value accrues to an entity that outlives every manager who books it. The deployed capital earns and is measured; the subscription maintains position and is not. A present-value calculation asks what the transaction returned; the ledger appears to ask whether the house is still where it needs to be. Those are different questions and only the second is defined over a trajectory.

This is a hypothesis and must be labelled one. Katō's evidence establishes the divergence and its bookkeeping mechanism; it does not establish the horizon. Three things would test it, and all are archival rather than theoretical. Whether the unpriced treatment of 掛金 persists across successions or changes with the manager — if it tracks the *ie* it should survive them. Whether kō membership correlates with commercial standing in places where the house held stores, rather than with returns. And whether the house ever exited a kō it scored as a loss, since a return-maximiser should and a position-maintainer should not.

The reason to pursue it here rather than elsewhere is that 中井源左衛門 is one of the best-documented houses of the period and the two evidence streams meet in one archive. Katō gives the loss-management side through the kō ledger — WP1's territory. D'Amico gives the entity-continuity side from the same archive — WP2's. It is unusual to be able to join the question of *what was being maximised* to the question of *what entity did the maximising* in one body of records, and this is a case where it can be done.

**D'Amico complicates the hypothesis in a productive way.** If the house is not one entity but a proliferating network of fictive households, some founded in partnership with unrelated merchants and divided into *kuchi* ([[The fictive household supplied partition and perpetuity without legal personality]]), then "the house's trajectory" is not a primitive. The trajectory whose ruin matters may be the network's rather than any single named household's — and the boundaries of that network were themselves a strategic choice, drawn to defeat creditors and lords across jurisdictions. That makes the fourth test the sharpest one: **whether the forty-six kō were entered by a single household or spread across the fictive entities.** Spread membership would say the kō were maintaining position for the network; concentrated membership would say they were an ordinary funding line for one store.

Two cautions. The reading must not slide into claiming the actors held a time-average criterion; it did not need to be held to operate ([[Time-average optimization is a survivorship property not an intention]]). And *ie* continuity is a claim about an institution transmitted as drafting practice and household precept, not about a lineage with a heritable disposition ([[Never let the units slide from instruments to populations]]).
