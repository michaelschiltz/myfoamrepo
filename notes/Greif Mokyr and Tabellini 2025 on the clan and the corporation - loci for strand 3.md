# Greif, Mokyr and Tabellini 2025 on the clan and the corporation — loci for strand 3

Avner Greif, Joel Mokyr and Guido Tabellini, *Two Paths to Prosperity: Culture and Institutions in Europe and China, 1000–2000* (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2025), 533 pp. ISBN 978-0-691-26594-0; e-book ISBN 978-0-691-26771-5.

**Read:** 6 November 2026, from the e-book PDF, which keeps the print pagination. All page references below are to the printed pages; in the PDF, the page index is the printed page plus nine. Every quotation and table figure below was checked against the page.

**Purpose:** to settle how HistorEE handles the book in strand 3, and in Objectives 1 and 2, where it touches them. It is the most authoritative current statement of the view the project inverts. The point is what it actually argues, where it is exposed, and what the project should and should not claim against it.

## 1. Verdict

The book is a large-scale narrative with almost no econometrics of its own. It rests on three supports:

1. a toy model whose result follows from its assumptions (ch. 3 appendix);
2. descriptive tabulations of a library catalogue of surviving genealogies (ch. 5, from Greif and Tabellini 2017);
3. other authors' regressions (Schulz 2022; Schulz et al. 2019; Zhang 2017; Fan et al. 2021; Kung and Wu 2025).

The large-scale claims are protected by how the argument is built: two cases, positive feedback, and an explicit setting-aside of causal identification. Where the book drops to the level of specific data, several measures fail a basic check of whether they measure what they claim (§§4, 7).

The book also concedes more than its reviewers report:

- that the clan/corporation contrast "should not be overdrawn" (123);
- that the clan "did not come to full bloom until the Ming Dynasty" (143);
- that lineage trusts had de facto legal personality, perpetual life, and a separation of ownership from management (170–71).

The case against it therefore has to be about how it absorbs that evidence, not about evidence it ignored.

## 2. How the argument is protected

- **It disclaims econometrics.** Its neighbours "mostly emphasize statistical correlations between cultural features and economic and political outcomes, we build our arguments from historical analysis and put social organizations at center stage" (13 n. 6). The book contains no regression of its own. Its one fitted line (fig. 10.2, 433) is replicated from Zhang (2017).
- **It sets causal identification aside.** p. 74 n. 3 quotes Bisin and Verdier (2017) with approval: under the joint evolution of culture and institutions, "the origin, and hence the causation, question loses most of its interest." Add positive feedback and two cases, and no observed outcome can count against the theory: any divergence is read as an initial difference amplified.
- **It hedges, then relies on the hedged evidence.** On Schulz's (2022) cross-sections linking prohibition of cousin marriage to democratic institutions: "Although these correlations are very robust, they could be driven by omitted variables, and hence they cannot be interpreted as strictly causal. Nevertheless, they suggest that the association … is a general phenomenon" (303).
- **The outcome is late; the cause is early.** "It remains true that much if not all of the income gap emerged during the Industrial Revolution. Yet it is important to emphasize that the roots of that reversal go back many centuries" (31). The roughly seven centuries between cause and effect are bridged only by the feedback mechanism.

## 3. The formal model (ch. 3 appendix, 88–94)

**3.1 The model contains no risk.** Material utility is *u = c + δg* (88–89): linear, deterministic, with a fixed endowment of 1, no stochastic term, no variance, no ruin and no horizon. Yet the text repeatedly names risk sharing and mutual insurance as core functions of both organisations: "protection, risk-sharing, land clearing and irrigation" (80); the lineage's common pool "facilitated risk-sharing arrangements" and was used to "diversify risk" (128); mutual insurance as the test case for membership rules (152–53); "risk sharing and poverty alleviation were typically left to the clans" (248). The book's only formal model of clan against corporation cannot represent what pooling does. Under multiplicative growth with an absorbing barrier, pooling changes the time-average growth rate. A model with additive utility and no shocks cannot register that by construction.

**3.2 The organisation is assumed to follow from the culture.** Condition (3.5) (91), F(0) + λ > δ(1 − α) > F(1), is chosen so that communitarians prefer the clan "even if clan size is minimal" and universalists prefer the corporation "even if clan size is maximal". Proposition 3.1 (full sorting) follows: "the size of the clan coincides with the fraction of the communitarian types in the population" (91). The body text says so plainly: "the prevailing social organization reflects the prevailing culture" (78).

**3.3 The historical content sits entirely in the starting value.** Condition (3.6) (92) adds economies of scale large enough to give the unstable interior point of Proposition 3.2. The combined condition (3.8) (93) requires

δ(1 − α) > F(1) > δ(1 − α) − (λ − γ) > F(0) > δ(1 − α) − λ.

Under the replicator dynamics (3.9), Proposition 3.3 (93) gives two stable states, at the extremes, and an unstable one between them. Which state society reaches is fixed by the initial share of communitarian types, x₀. So the model turns the historical question into an initial condition, and the narrative about the Church supplies that condition. The model cannot fail; it formalises hysteresis, not the divergence.

**3.4 Clan enforcement assumes a stationary repeated game.** The clan's lower enforcement cost comes from its ability to "exploit ongoing relationships among its members" (89). This is the stationarity assumption the project has already challenged in the Maghribi case.

**3.5 Minor.** Two different equations are both numbered (3.5): the sorting condition (91) and the fitness difference Δ(x) (92). The dynamics then refer to Δ "as defined in equation (3.5)" (93).

## 4. Dating the clan (ch. 5, 139–49)

**4.1 The text concedes the Ming.** The authors concede that the system "did not come to full bloom until the Ming Dynasty" (143). They cite Kung and Wu (2025), reporting that the 1536 ritual reform "led to a sudden increase in the construction of ancestral halls" (140). Kung and Wu's own figure is that 98.7% of 18,685 halls built over eight centuries date from after 1536; GMT do not quote it. They absorb the result by reading the hall as "the culmination of a long process" (140–41), and they dismiss Chen's (2017) later dating as "not altogether persuasive" (142).

**4.2 The early dating rests on retrospective claims.** The dataset is the union catalogue of surviving genealogies, *Zhongguo jiapu zongmu* 中国家谱总目 (Wang 2008): "about 51,200 observations regarding 38,429 clans" (143). 98.6% of recorded updates fall between 1700 and 2005 (fig. 5.1, 148). Table 5.1 (145) dates the transition by the dynasty of the clan's claimed apical ancestor. That variable records claims made in Ming–Qing–Republican compilations about Tang–Song ancestors.

The authors themselves list "fabricating evidence of common descent" among the purposes of genealogies (143). They then use the claimed dates as data. Survival bias (against early dates) and prestige bias (towards them) are said to pull in opposite directions (143); neither is measured.

**4.3 The founders column, the closer proxy for when the organisation formed, says Ming.**

| Period | Claimed ancestors (n = 10,852) | Founders (n = 22,141) |
|---|---|---|
| Tang + Five Dynasties + Song | 61% | 26% |
| Yuan | 10% | 12% |
| Ming + Qing | 27% | 61% |

Source: table 5.1 (145), from Greif and Tabellini (2017). Pre-Tang rows omitted.

The authors read the founders column as branches of earlier lineages (144). That reading assumes the early origin the column was supposed to test.

**4.4 The regional distribution is defended circularly.** Table 5.2 (147): 78% of dated clans trace to the East. Note 30 (146) concedes that "only in the East were there early scholarly efforts to preserve genealogies before the Cultural Revolution". It answers that "this early preservation was due to the process of clan formation itself": the bias is dismissed by assuming the conclusion.

**4.5 Most clans held no common property.** Table 5.3 (149) rests on 485 Ming genealogies (Yangtze 417, rest 68). Only 21–28% of clans held common property (weighted average 27% Yangtze, 21.6% rest of China). So most clans in their own data had no trust, and therefore no asset pool that could be shielded. The element that resembles a corporation is the minority case.

## 5. Two meanings of "corporation"

**5.1 The cultural argument uses a loose definition.** "Corporation" is a voluntary association of unrelated individuals, and "this term should not be interpreted as a precise legal definition" (152).

**5.2 The economic payoff uses a precise one.** Following Harris (2020, ch. 9) and Dari-Mattiacci et al. (2017), the modern business corporation has: (1) legal personality; (2) collective decision rules and representation; (3) joint-stock equity and transferable shares; (4) capital lock-in; (5) limited liability and entity shielding; and, from Harris, (6) protection from the sovereign (158).

"All the elements …, and in particular capital lock-in, emerged for the first time in Europe at the beginning of the seventeenth century" (159). Following Harris, the cause is the demand created by transoceanic trade, not culture (159).

**5.3 The components are conceded on both sides.**

- Medieval private ventures already had "equity financing with profit-sharing, elements of limited liability, entity shielding, and some transferability of shares", but only as "private contracts between individuals who knew each other" (159).
- Clans and corporations "exhibited a de facto legal personality … recognized by the law and could last in perpetuity" (170).
- Lineage trusts already separated ownership from management (171).
- Commenda-like clan partnerships were "essential to the evolution of long-distance trade in China" (133, citing McDermott 2022).
- Late Qing ancestral estates were run as "hard-nosed business corporations" (151, quoting Watson 1982).

**5.4 What remains as the difference** is the membership rule, outside equity, chartering, limited liability and free transfer of shares (128, 162–63). That is a list of separable components, which is the project's decomposition. The book carries out half of the decomposition and then stops at the cultural label.

**5.5 Counter-cases are handled by changing the criterion.** The Toulouse mills had limited liability and tradeable shares in the fourteenth century. Note 42 (159) sets them aside because "they promoted cooperation between neighbors, not strangers". That criterion is not on the feature list of 158. Neither Sicard (1953) nor Le Bris, Goetzmann and Pouget (2019) is cited. Ruskola (2000) is dismissed as "little more than an exercise in strawmanship" (128 n. 8).

**5.6 A concession worth taking.** Participation in the lineage organisation "was hereditary, and typically heirs could not renounce their inheritance even if debts exceeded liabilities [*sic*]" (163, citing Ruskola 2000). This is liability carried across generations with no exit: a ruin barrier with no firewall. It is a candidate coding for the lineage-trust form (§9).

## 6. Persistence and identification (chs. 7 and 10)

These are WP3's exemplars of space-for-time substitution: cross-sectional variation read as change over time within one society.

- **Church exposure** (Schulz 2022), as reported at 302 n. 89, is the number of years a city lay within 100 km of the nearest Western bishop after 550. Bishops were largely seated in former Roman *civitas* capitals, so the measure also tracks continuity of Roman urban settlement. The outcome, becoming a commune, plausibly depends on that continuity directly.
- **Clan "persistence"** (fig. 10.2, 433) plots log numbers of genealogies before 1950 against after 1980 across 272 prefectures. The caption mentions no population normalisation. If none was applied, the slope partly reflects prefecture size and how well each prefecture's holdings were catalogued [verify against Zhang 2017, fig. 3].
- **The instrument in Fan et al. (2021)**, reported at 435, is the southward migration after the fall of the Northern Song, used to instrument modern clan strength in a regression of contract-intensive industrial location (2007). The exclusion restriction requires that this migration affects twenty-first-century industrial structure only through clans. The same migration reshaped population density and the economic geography of the lower Yangzi.
- **Most of the modern regressions the book cites on clans and firms** (Fan et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2021; Zhang 2017; 435–36) measure clan strength by genealogy counts, so they inherit the catalogue problem in §4. Xie and Yuan (2022) use the share of a county's population that kept genealogies before 1949 (436).

## 7. Micro-level checks

**7.1 The surname test (157–58).** The book infers that "because English marriages were between people with different surnames, this also points to far fewer within-lineage marriages." The test cannot discriminate between the two cases:

- From the Tang code onwards, Chinese law prohibited marriage between persons of the same surname (同姓不婚; two years' penal servitude in the Tang code, a prohibition carried into the Ming and Qing codes) [verify article numbers in Johnson 1997 and Jiang 2005]. The test therefore gives China the same reading.
- Under patrilineal surnames, first cousins through a mother or a father's sister always carry different surnames, so surname difference at marriage cannot detect cousin marriage in either society.
- No Chinese comparison figure is supplied for the London 1541 statistic (top ten surnames, 7.85%; 157). Chinese surname concentration reflects a small stock of surnames, not clan organisation.
- Sources for the English figures include two hobbyist websites (ellipsis.cx; britishsurnames.co.uk; 157 nn. 40–41).

**7.2 Venice and Genoa (157).**

- **Venice 1090:** the 1090 document (127 individuals, 91 surnames) predates stable hereditary surnames; it measures naming practice as much as kinship.
- **The Venetian Great Council:** the lists for 1261–1296/7 (392 individuals, 119 surnames) record the decades leading to the 1297 *Serrata*, which made membership hereditary. Note 38 (157) concedes the *serrata* in passing, citing Puga and Trefler (2014).
- **Genoa:** "136 families constituted the top nonaristocratic merchants from 1375 to 1450" (Van Doosselaere 2009). That period is the height of the *alberghi*: associations, attested from 1383, in which families, including unrelated ones, took a common surname. The Giustiniani were the shareholders of the Chios *maona* (Kirk 2005, 25). A European share-holding company thus took on a fictive kin name. That is the "contractual lineage" GMT treat as Chinese (127–28). The *alberghi* are not mentioned in the book.

**7.3 Urbanisation (38–39).** China's urbanisation is 3.1% around 1000 in table 2.4 (Maddison 2007; cities over 10,000), with Europe printed as 0. It is 11–12% for 1109–1205 in table 2.5 (Xu, van Leeuwen and van Zanden 2018). The tables are a page apart and not reconciled.

**7.4 References.**

- p. 31 n. 5 cites "Solar's revised figures (table 2.1)"; table 2.1 (35) is Chinese tax revenue.
- *One Quarter of Humanity* is listed under Lee alone; Wang Feng is the co-author.
- Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) is given as *Journal of Economic History* 78(4), "pp. 1–46" [verify printed pagination].
- Solar's (2021) critique of the Broadberry–Guan–Li Chinese GDP series is cited. Broadberry, Guan and Li's (2021) "restatement", which dates the divergence to about 1700, is not.

## 8. What the book does not engage

Checked by full-text search of the e-book on 6 November 2026.

- Zelin (2005) on the Zigong salt shareholdings: "Zigong" does not occur.
- Sicard (1953) and Le Bris, Goetzmann and Pouget (2019) on the Bazacle.
- The Genoese *alberghi* and the Chios *maona*.
- Broadberry, Guan and Li (2021).
- Le Bris and Gay (2023), which tests Todd's diffusion of family innovations against Church exposure as predictors of kinship intensity. Todd (1987) is cited for the typology only.

## 9. Implications for HistorEE, with recommendations

**R1. Part 1, strand 3: name the book once, early, as the strongest current statement of the view the project inverts. Recommended.** Use three points with page anchors:

1. the riskless model (88–94);
2. the switch between the two meanings of "corporation" (152 against 158–59);
3. the dating by claimed ancestors (table 5.1, 145, against 143).

Do not attack the authors and do not contest the GDP dating, which belongs to Broadberry and Goldstone.

**R2. Retire the phrase "category error" from the earlier recommendation of 6 November.** Replace it with "a switch between two definitions". The book concedes that the components overlap (§5.3); the vulnerable point is that the cultural argument and the economic payoff use different definitions of "corporation". **Recommended.**

**R3. Part 2, WP3: cite 302 n. 89, 433 and 435 as exemplars of space-for-time substitution.** They are the clearest available cases from a Nobel-laureate synthesis. **Recommended.**

**R4. The model gap (§3.1) is the most valuable point for Objective 1.** State it as: "the book's only formal model of clan against corporation has additive utility and no shocks, and so cannot represent the risk-sharing function it assigns to both". This statement needs no branded vocabulary. **Recommended for Part 1.**

**R5. HistorEE_codebooks: two candidate forms, for a later coding pass, not now.**

1. The lineage trust (*yizhuang* / *tang*), with the heir's inability to renounce (163) as a liability-variable cell.
2. The Genoese *albergo*, as a European organisation that took on fictive kinship.

Candidates only; no coding implied.

**R6. A review essay, not the proposal, should carry §7.** The micro-level checks are strong, but they are critique of the book, not part of the project's own case. The proposal should carry only R1–R4.

## 10. Corrections to the 6 November 2026 summary of the reviews

- The ancestral-hall paper previously cited as "The Rise of the Chinese Clan (ResearchGate; authorship unverified)" is Kung and Wu (2025), SSRN 5100471. **GMT cite and engage it** (140–41). The earlier implication that the book leaves it unanswered was wrong.
- The earlier claim that GMT dismiss China's merchant associations as mere clans holds for Goldstone's reading, but it needs qualifying against the book's own concessions (123, 127–28, 133).

## 11. Reception

As of 6 November 2026.

- **Van Zanden, EH.Net, December 2025.** He calls the book a possible "conclusion to the debate"; he also wrote the jacket blurb ("dream team"). His reservations: the family firm dominated the economic sphere; doubt that a Chinese path to prosperity is shown.
- **Goldstone, guest post on Koyama, *How the World Became Rich*, 26 February 2026.** A rebuttal of van Zanden: the California School is misrepresented; the 1700 dating from Broadberry, Guan and Li (2021); partnerships, not joint-stock companies, financed British industrialisation, 1720–1824.
- **Seabright, *TLS*, June 2026.** The marriage–kinship step is contested; no index entry for slavery.
- **Marein, *Independent Review* 31(2), Fall 2026.** Variation within Europe (Iberia, Eastern Europe).
- **Rutar, *Political Economy, Stats, and Society*, 30 September 2026.** Maddison parity in 1000; Le Bris and Gay (2023); the Little Divergence.
- **Root, SSRN review essay, November 2025.** Not read.
- **Mainly favourable:** Hodgson, *LSE Review of Books*, July 2026; Herndon, *Asian Review of Books*, January 2026; Begović, *Anali Pravnog fakulteta u Beogradu* 2026/3 (not read).
- **No review by a China specialist found.**
