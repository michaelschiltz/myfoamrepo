# Latin law lets the agent mix commended capital and Hanafi law does not

**Status: qualified (2026-10-13), after five tests** (rulings 1–6 of `records/RULINGS-latin-hanafi-mixing-tests-2026-10-13.md` in HistorEE_codebooks). **The title holds:** Latin law lets the agent mix commended capital and Ḥanafī law does not. **The explanation does not hold as first written.** The two traditions share the rule that the owners' consent makes a mixture common, and that unconsented mixing of coins ends the owner's title (D. 46.3.78; Abū Ḥanīfa, *Mabsūṭ* 11, 92, 111). They differ in **who is presumed to consent**: Latin commenda law supplies the investor's consent by default, Ḥanafī law requires it expressly (*iʿmal fīhi bi-raʾyika*). The *ribā* step is withdrawn (test 1). The coded Latin–Islamic split is withdrawn: the Mālikī *qirāḍ* lets the agent mix (test 2), and `qirad CI5` is now `commingled` (ruling 3). Outcomes: test 1 qualifies; test 2 counts against; test 3 supports a restatement on the law of mixtures, which test 5 cuts back on the Latin side; test 4 qualifies (Venetian statutory silence); test 5 qualifies. The property-law difference that survives is Mālikī deposit law, where like-with-like mixing without consent leaves co-ownership.

**The finding it explains.** After the Italian re-code, the commenda family separates on `CI5`, segregation from the holder's capital, along a Latin–Islamic line: *[2026-10-13: no longer. The `qirad` row is Mālikī, and Mālikī law lets the agent mix his own money with the *qirāḍ* unstipulated and without liability (*Mudawwana* 3, 641, 645); `qirad CI5` is now `commingled` (ruling 3, `OF-R0224`). Segregation by default is attested only in Ḥanafī law, which no row codes.]*

- **Genoa commingles by default.** Cassinese drafts *implicare separatim* only as an express clause.
- **Pisa joins a valued contribution to the traveller's stock at once**: *cum hentica statim communicet* (Bonaini II, 885). Its land rubric repeats the rule: *cum alio suo havere … misceat*, with profit divided *per libram* (898).
- **Catalonia lets the commendatary mix the comanda with his own money unless the investor restricted him.** Gain and loss are then divided *per sou e per liura* (*Consolat*, cap. 215).
- **Ḥanafī law makes the *muḍārib* liable and voids the *muḍāraba* for the mixed sum** if he mixes without the investor's authority, *iʿmal fīhi bi-raʾyika* (al-Sarakhsī, *al-Mabsūṭ* 22, 48).

See [[Comanda capital was mixed with the agent's own unless the contract said otherwise]] for the Catalan reading and its correction.

**The hypothesis.** Both traditions legitimate the investor's profit through his continued ownership of the capital. They differ in what their law of mixtures does to that ownership.

- **Both rest profit on ownership.** Aquinas allows the commendator his profit *tanquam de re sua*, because he *non transfert dominium pecuniae suae* (*ST* II-II 78.2 ad 5, quoted in Pryor 1977, 34). In the *qirāḍ*, whatever the agent buys with the capital belongs to the investor, and his share is lawful as the growth of his own property, not as a return on a loan (Shaybānī, via Udovitch, cited in Pryor 1977, 33). This is the same move on both sides: profit without *ribā* or usury requires that ownership never pass.
- **In Roman law, mixing preserves ownership as a share.** Goods of two owners mixed by consent become co-owned in proportion to the quantities, by *confusio* or *commixtio* (Inst. 2.1.27–28; not read this session). *[Corrected 2026-10-13, test 5: true for consensual mixing (Inst. 2.1.27–28; D. 41.1.7.8; D. 6.1.5 pr.) and for blending by chance (D. 41.1.7.9). Grain mixed by one owner without the other's consent is not common; each sues for his quantity (Inst. 2.1.28). **Coins mixed beyond telling apart become the holder's**, the owner keeping an action for theft: *si mixti essent, ita ut discerni non possent, eius fieri qui accepit* (D. 46.3.78). For the commenda's money, then, the Latin default rests on the investor's consent, not on a Roman rule that unconsented mixing preserves title.]* The investor's goods become a proportional share of the mass, which is exactly the *per libram* division the Latin sources prescribe. His profit is still *de re sua*. The statutes add a priority over the traveller's estate (the Genoese Breve, c. VI). Mixing costs the investor nothing he relies on, so the law can leave the decision to the traveller.
- **In Ḥanafī law, unauthorised mixing by a trustee extinguishes ownership.** The *muḍārib* is an *amīn* ([[The amana-daman structure refuses to infer fault from loss]]). A trustee who mixes the owner's property inseparably with his own is treated as having consumed it: he owes an equivalent, and the owner's property becomes a claim against him. A profit share on such a claim would be *ribā*. That is why mixing without authority both creates liability and voids the *muḍāraba* for that sum. This mechanism is inferred from the rule on deposits and has not been read for the *muḍāraba* itself. *[Corrected 2026-10-13, test 1: read in al-Sarakhsī, the mechanism is **Abū Ḥanīfa's**, not the school's. On his view, the *ẓāhir* of the school, mixing makes the owner's property *mustahlakan ḥukman* and the mixture the mixer's (*Mabsūṭ* 11, 92, 111), and an insolvent *muḍārib*'s investor ranks pro rata with the creditors, *ṣārat al-amāna daynan ʿalayhi* (22, 143). Abū Yūsuf and Muḥammad hold the mixer liable *but not owner*: the owner may elect co-ownership of the mixture (*sharika*) or claim the equivalent (11, 92, 111; 22, 143). **The *ribā* step is withdrawn**: no passage read invokes *ribā*. The text's ground is ownership. Profit is *namāʾ al-māl*, the owner's unless stipulated otherwise (22, 144). Liability transfers the capital to the mixer, so he owns its profit, which is *kasb khabīth* to be given in alms (22, 133, 158), and the *muḍāraba* lapses because its condition, that the capital be an *amāna*, fails (22, 48, 133). Al-Sarakhsī states the ground disjunctively: mixing attracts *ḍamān* because it amounts to consuming the owner's property *or* to imposing co-ownership on it without his consent (22, 132–133; the second limb alone at 22, 39). The wrong is the act: mixing that arises *ḥukman*, through a single purchase with both funds or mixed proceeds, is lawful and leaves co-ownership pro rata (22, 48, 133–134).]*

**The exceptions point the same way.** Ḥanafī law accepts mixing that the investor authorises: consensual mixing creates a co-ownership the law permits. So it does not forbid co-ownership. It denies the trustee the power to impose it. *[2026-10-13: test 1 makes this the main mechanism. Al-Sarakhsī's ground for the *muḍāraba* rule is *ījāb al-sharika … ʿalā wajh lam yarḍa bihi rabb al-māl* (22, 39), with consumption as the alternative limb (22, 132–133). On the companions' view the investor in a mixed fund keeps a pro-rata share, as in Latin law, and the traditions differ only in who may decide to mix. Accidental mixing in the depositary's chest makes the mass co-owned *bi-qadr milkihimā*, with loss shared pro rata (11, 110).]* Conversely, the Latin rule closest to the Ḥanafī one is the *Consolat*'s cap. 219, which bars mixing a comanda into a company's common stock without notice. There, mixing would dilute the investor among other people's claims, not just the agent's. On this reading each tradition assigns the decision to mix according to whether mixing threatens the investor's title.

**Why it matters for the census.** `CI5` was added as description and kept out of the asset-partitioning set. *[2026-10-13: after ruling 3 the census shows no `CI5` split in the commenda family; the paragraph below now concerns a Ḥanafī divergence the census does not code.]* If the hypothesis holds, the Latin–Islamic split on `CI5` is not a difference in commercial practice. It is a difference in property law, which the census would otherwise read as a difference in how the forms operate. That bears on the substitution thesis: in the *qirāḍ* the investor's protection is title, and in the Latin forms it is a pro-rata share plus a priority over the traveller's estate.

## Where this is soft

- **Two traditions, not many cases.** Genoa, Pisa and Catalonia share a Roman-law substrate and count as one datum against the Ḥanafī one: parallelism, not convergence. *[2026-10-13: the Mālikī *qirāḍ* sides with the Latin datum (test 2), so the split is Latin and Mālikī against Ḥanafī, one datum each, with al-Shāfiʿī silent in what was read.]*
- **Law, not practice.** The `CI5` cells code the default rule. The Geniza shows *qirāḍ* contracts in which the agent also invested (Goitein, via Pryor 1977, 30), and Genoese notaries drafted *separatim* against their own default.
- **Key steps are unread.** The Roman texts on mixture and the Ḥanafī rule that mixing counts as consumption have not been read first-hand. *[2026-10-13: the Ḥanafī rule is now read (test 1); it is Abū Ḥanīfa's, and contested within the school. The Roman texts remain for test 5.]* *[2026-10-13: the Roman texts are read (test 5). Still unread: the Roman texts on deposited money used by the depositary (D. 16.3.25.1, D. 12.1.9.9, D. 12.1.10), a later Shāfiʿī statement on the* qirāḍ, *and the Venetian acts.]*

## Tests

1. **Al-Sarakhsī on mixing in the deposit chapter of the *Mabsūṭ*:** does inseparable mixing by a depositary count as consumption and shift liability? **Outcome (2026-10-13): qualifies.** Liability shifts for all three jurists. Consumption and the owner's reduction to a creditor are Abū Ḥanīfa's view; the companions give the owner the option of co-ownership. The deposit chapter itself grounds liability in the owner's lost access to *ʿayn milkihi* (11, 110). The *ribā* step is withdrawn; the vol. 22 clusters bar the act of mixing, not commingled holdings.
2. **Mālikī and Shāfiʿī law on mixing in the *qirāḍ*:** Pryor 1977, 30 cites Mālik's objection to the investor profiting from capital he did not provide, a *ribā* concern adjacent to this one. **Outcome (2026-10-13): counts against.** Mālikī law lets the agent mix his own money with the *qirāḍ* without the investor's leave and without liability, and Ibn al-Qāsim calls it the right course: *al-ṣawāb … an yakhliṭahumā jamīʿan thumma yashtarī bihimā jamīʿan*; Mālik, *lā taḍmanu lahu* (*Mudawwana* 3, 641; two investors' funds, 645). What Mālik bars is *stipulating* the agent's contribution, because the investor *qad jarra ilā nafsihi manfaʿat māl ghayr mālihi* (648): profit rests on ownership here too, but the ground is a stipulated benefit, and *ribā* is not named. Pryor's "usurious character" (n. 85, after Gaiani) mistranslates *li-stighzār al-shirāʾ*, and his claim that the jurists did not discuss the bilateral *qirāḍ* is contradicted by the *Mudawwana* and by al-Sarakhsī (22, 32, 140). The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* has no mixing rule; al-Shāfiʿī states none in the passages read. The bar is Ḥanafī, not Islamic.
3. **Other Islamic trust contracts, agency and deposit:** the hypothesis predicts the same rule. **Outcome (2026-10-13): supports, the hypothesis restated.** Each school's trustee rule is one rule across contracts. Ḥanafī law makes the mixer liable in deposit (*Mabsūṭ* 11, 110), in the *muḍāraba*, and for the agent, whose collected money *kāna bi-manzilat al-wadīʿa ʿinda al-wakīl* (19, 74; by assimilation, no mixing rule in *Kitāb al-wakāla*). Mālikī law lets the depositary mix like with like without liability, *innamā hiya darāhim wa-darāhim, fa-li-hādhā minhā bi-qadr darāhimihi* (Ibn al-Qāsim, *Mudawwana* 4, 434), co-owners pro rata in what remains (435), as in its *qirāḍ*. Al-Shāfiʿī makes the depositary liable for inseparable mixing (*Umm*, *al-wadīʿa*); his *qirāḍ* rule is unread. **Restated:** the commenda family's mixing default follows each tradition's law of mixtures for fungibles held in trust: where like-with-like mixing leaves the owner a pro-rata share the agent may mix, where it makes the mixer liable he may not. *MS's reservation (2026-10-13): he suspects the Mālikī deposit and* qirāḍ *rules, which give different reasons (safekeeping; avoiding a price fall), coincide by chance.* *[2026-10-13, test 5: on the Latin side the restatement does not hold. Roman law makes unconsented mixing of coins end the owner's title, as Abū Ḥanīfa does, so the Latin–Ḥanafī difference lies in the default allocation of consent, not in the law of mixtures.]*
4. **The Venetian *colleganza*:** the hypothesis predicts commingling *per libram*. Besta & Predelli 1901 has not been read for mixing. **Outcome (2026-10-13): qualifies.** Besta & Predelli 1901 has no Venetian rule on mixing before 1242, either way. Ranieri Dandolo c. 16 (c. 1202–1205; p. 251) fixes the unilateral *colleganza*'s division of profit (a quarter to the taker, or as the charter says). Tiepolo 1229 c. 16 (p. 273) requires an itemised account, *seriatim … qualiter egerit de hoc quod habuit in colegantia*, which is a duty to account, not to segregate. Venice adds nothing to the Latin datum on mixing; the acts (Morozzo della Rocca & Lombardo 1940, not held) would settle it.
5. **Inst. 2.1.27–28 and D. 41.1.7.8–9 on *confusio* and *commixtio*:** confirm that the Latin *per libram* default tracks the Roman co-ownership rule. **Outcome (2026-10-13): qualifies.** Consent makes the mass common (Inst. 2.1.27–28; D. 41.1.7.8; D. 6.1.5 pr.). Without consent the result depends on mode and matter: blending by chance gives co-ownership; grain mixed by one owner gives separate claims for each owner's quantity; silver melted into a mass gives shares by weight (D. 6.1.3.2), the fraudulent mixer being liable for theft (D. 6.1.4). Coins mixed beyond telling apart become the holder's (D. 46.3.78). The Latin *per libram* default tracks the consensual rule, the commenda supplying consent by contract or statute (Pisa's *statim communicet* echoes Inst. 2.1.28's *communicata*).
