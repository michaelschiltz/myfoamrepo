# Martinez Gijon on the comenda in Spanish law

Source note for José Martínez Gijón's three studies, which are the doctrinal treatment of the comanda for the Crown of Aragon and the only systematic one:

- "La comenda en el Derecho español. I. La comenda-depósito", *AHDE* 34 (1964), 31–140 (Zotero U7ASLIDI);
- "II. La comenda mercantil", *AHDE* 36 (1966), 379–456 (XLNCQXGB);
- "La comenda y el transporte de mercancías en el Derecho español de la Baja Edad Media", *Historia. Instituciones. Documentos* 1 (1974), 263–274 (MWTFU4CG), his paper to the Datini week of 1973.

The 1964 and 1966 PDFs (BOE) are image-only and were OCR'd on 2026-10-07. For 1966 the printed page is the PDF page + 378. At the time of writing only pp. 379–410 (sections A and B) had been read; section C on the *Consolat* and section D on sixteenth-century Seville were not. The 1964 article has not been read.

**Method.** He codes from the thirteenth- and fourteenth-century acts published by Sayous, Carreras y Candi, Miret i Sans, García Sanz and Verlinden, quoting the clauses. He also uses Jaume I's provisions of 1269 and 1271, *Recognoverunt proceres* 69 (1283), the *Costums de Tortosa* and the *Consolat de Mar*. He notes that the Cantabrian and Atlantic sources contain no comanda at all (1966, 381–383).

## Findings relied on (1966 unless stated)

- **Deposit against trade.** The deposit and the mercantile comanda share the commendatary's confession of receipt, the renunciation of the *exceptio non numeratae pecuniae*, and unilateral guarantees by the commendatary. They differ in the trading the commendatary must do (383–384). In a deposit of fungibles the depositary answers even for mischance (1974, 273), and the deposit "most often hides a usurious loan" (406).
- **Unilateral predominates.** The form in which the commendatary contributes neither capital nor goods "es la que predomina en el Derecho español"; bilateral documents are "escasos" (386).
- **Risk.** In the unilateral maritime comanda the risks fall on the capitalist, outward and back: "ad tuum resecum et fortunam", "per mare et per terram et per aquam dulcem" (397). Theft, piracy, wreck, average and jettison are his (398). In bilateral comandas, by sea or land, losses are divided in the same proportion as profits (398).
- **Profit shares.** Unilateral maritime acts give 3/4 to the investor and 1/4 to the commendatary (394, 1232); one act of 1256 gives 2/3 and 1/3. Comandas without travel vary: in 1230 the agents take 2/3 with no capital of their own, for one year, with *fideiussores*; in a 1261 act the agent who also contributed takes only 1/3 (395–397).
- **The local comanda.** Craftsmen (butchers, shoemakers) receive money "in comanda ad societatem" for their trade, 1261 and 1273. Profit and loss are shared, which he reads as a bilateral form with the craft as contribution, and "not substantially different" from the maritime bilateral comanda (387–388).
- **Duration and liquidation.** A maritime comanda is liquidated at the end of the voyage. Broader or local ones end at the term or at the capitalist's will: "hec vero comanda sive societas duret quamdiu tibi … placuerit" (399).
- **Guarantees.** Failure to render account brings into play the guarantees, "personales y reales a la vez", which are "usually included" in both unilateral and bilateral acts: "obligamus … nos et omnia bona mobilia et inmobilia habita et habenda" (401).
- **Nature.** He surveys the Italian and German literature (Lastig, Goldschmidt, Chiaudano, Astuti, Silberschmidt, Sayous) and concludes that the comanda creates a credit relation, "ex causa mutui, commandae seu depositi". Its trading and its risk and profit clauses legitimise and blur its "primitive feneraticia nature" (402–406).
