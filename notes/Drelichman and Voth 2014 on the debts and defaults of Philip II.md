# Drelichman and Voth 2014 on the debts and defaults of Philip II

Source note for *Lending to the Borrower from Hell* and its underlying articles. The book reconstructs Castile's annual fiscal accounts for 1566–96 and codes 438 short-term loan contracts (*asientos*) from the Archivo General de Simancas. It is the evidentiary base for six claims in this vault; the claims are elsewhere, the apparatus is here.

## What the book establishes

Philip II's four payment stops (1557, 1560, 1575, 1596) were liquidity crises, not insolvency. Revenue rose in line with expenditure, the debt burden did not explode, and the reign ended with debt at roughly the multiple of revenue at which it began (2014: 4, 120). Lending was profitable: virtually every banker made money, repeat business ran at 85.4 per cent of volume, and a banker who lent once had an 88 per cent chance of lending again (149). Braudel's account, in which each default ruined a nationality of dupes who were replaced by the next, fails on the composition of the lender pool, which is stable across the 1575 break (148–49).

The two mechanisms the book credits are lender coalition and contractual contingency. The Genoese lent in overlapping syndicates, acted as one during moratoria, and refused royal side deals (6, 134). Contingency clauses made repayment conditional on verifiable states of the world (chapter 7).
