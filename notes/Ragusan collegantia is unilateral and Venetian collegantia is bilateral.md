# Ragusan collegantia is unilateral and Venetian collegantia is bilateral

The same word names two different contracts in two cities eighty sea-miles apart, and the second city took the word from the first.

In Venetian documents of the thirteenth century *collegantia* is used **exclusively for the bilateral *commenda*** — both parties invest, one travels — and the unilateral form has no designation at all (Held 2025, 9, following Pryor 1977, 10). Dubrovnik was under Venetian rule, traded intensively with the Serenissima, and took the term. Yet Held's conclusion from all 53 relevant notarial contracts, 1278–1301, is that "the contract of *collegantia* in the late medieval law of Dubrovnik is a local variant of the **unilateral** *commenda*" (23).

## Why the name did not carry the structure

Because in Dubrovnik the name was carrying something else. Held's decisive observation is that *collegantia* and the clause *salvos in terra* are **mutually exclusive** in the documents, and both stand immediately after the sum advanced. Where the risk sits on the commendator the contract is called a *collegantia*; where the tractator must repay "even if it was lost due to… extraordinary, unforeseen events" it is not, whatever else it retains. Hence: "designation of a contract as *collegantia* would thus necessarily imply a particular allocation of risks" (23).

So the term is a **risk-allocation label**, not a partnership-structure label. Venice used it for who invests; Dubrovnik used it for who bears the peril. Nothing was mistranslated — the word was repurposed to answer the question that mattered locally.

## Two consequences

**For coding.** A census row named for a contemporary term inherits whichever question that term was answering, and the answer varies by city. This is [[Natie is a false cognate]] in a second corpus: the unit has to be the clause structure, never the name on it. Held is explicit that in this material "terminology is sometimes used inadequately and elements of various essentially different contracts applied interchangeably", and that systematising it "according to clear-cut juridical and scholarly categories" is "hard, if not impossible".

**For the risk-allocation axis.** It is evidence that the peril-bearer distinction is a **contemporary legal category** and not an analyst's overlay. Merchants encoded it in the name of the instrument. Hoover (1926) finds the Genoese notaries doing the cognate thing a century earlier — taking care over "the proper incidence of the marine risk upon the different parties".

## Where this is soft

Held's corpus is one city and twenty-three years, and three notaries of markedly unequal competence (one of them a parish priest). The mutual exclusivity is his inference from co-occurrence, offered against an older reading — Danilović's — in which unspecified risk followed the profit shares. He gives the count, not a statistical test.

**And the philological claim about Venice is contested in practice, if not in print.** Held's "used exclusively for the contract of bilateral commenda" comes from Pryor 1977, 10. Two recent quantitative studies of the Venetian corpus use the word the other way. Puga & Trefler (2014, 12) describe "the colleganza" as the contract in which the sedentary merchant supplies all the capital and the traveller only labour — the unilateral form. Merelo-Guervós & Molinari (2025), working 381 contracts from 1073 to 1342, treat *colleganza* as the umbrella and reserve *societas maris* for the bilateral variant, adding that "by the early thirteenth century, bilateral contracts had practically disappeared" and the Tiepolo Statutes of 1242 regulate only the unilateral type.

These are not straightforwardly contradictory — Pryor is describing what the *documents* say, the economists are using a modern label for a family — but they cannot both be safe to cite. **Anyone quoting "Venetian *collegantia* means the bilateral form" needs Pryor's page, not a secondary chain**, and should note that the term's modern usage has drifted the opposite way from its documentary one. The irony is worth keeping: this note is about a name that failed to carry its structure across eighty sea-miles, and the same name has now failed to carry it across a disciplinary boundary.
