# A stationary Gaussian AR(1) has no arrow of time

A strictly stationary Gaussian process is time-reversible: the joint law of $(x_t, x_{t+1}, \dots, x_{t+k})$ equals that of the reversed sequence, because a Gaussian law is fixed by its autocovariances and these are symmetric in the lag. For linear processes driven by independent innovations, reversibility essentially characterises the Gaussian case (Weiss 1975) [verify the exact scope of the theorem].

Clark's latent-status model, in its standard linear-Gaussian form, is such a process. Its account of eight centuries is statistically indifferent to the direction of time: run the lineages backwards and the data look the same. The ancestors of an elite cohort regress towards the mean as their descendants do — the reciprocal regression Galton already noted ([[Regression was invented as a stationarity argument]]). A model with this property can describe a symmetric correlation structure across generations. It cannot by itself carry a claim about the propagation of an initial condition forward in time, because nothing in it distinguishes propagation from retrodiction. Contrast [[Retrodiction fails on non-invertibility not on induction]], where the asymmetry between the two directions is the whole point.

Two consequences. First, a test is available: time-irreversibility tests on lineage or surname-group series check directly whether the stationary linear model is adequate, and a detectable arrow refutes it. Second, an arrow, where present, must come from non-Gaussian shocks, from absorption, or from a law that changes with regimes — each a historical object. See [[Time-homogeneity not stationarity is what removes time]] and [[An absorbing barrier breaks ergodicity by construction]].
