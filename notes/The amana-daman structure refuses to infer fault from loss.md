# The amana-daman structure refuses to infer fault from loss

Islamic law sorts everyone holding another's property into *yad al-amāna*, the hand of trust, or *yad al-ḍamān*, the hand of guaranty. The *amīn* owes an obligation of **care, not of result**: if the property perishes without his fault, the loss lies where it falls, on the owner. The *ḍāmin* owes the thing or its value regardless of fault. The depositary (*wadīʿa*), the agent (*wakīl*), the lessee, the partner and the *muḍārib* hold on *amāna*; the borrower of fungibles holds under *ḍamān*, as, punitively, does the usurper (*ghāṣib*).

The trustee's exemption lapses and *ḍamān* attaches on proof of *taʿaddī* — transgression, doing what was not permitted — of negligence, or of *mukhālafa*, contravention of stipulated instructions.

## The claim

The structure is a rule about **who bears the epistemic burden of an unobservable state**. After a catastrophic loss the question — nature, or the agent? — is frequently unanswerable, because the ship sank and there is nothing left to inspect. The law does not attempt to improve the inference. It **refuses to run it**. Loss as such is evidence of nothing; liability attaches only on a conduct fact, establishable independently of how the venture came out.

That is the substitution of state-verification for outcome-inference, executed at the level of doctrine rather than of practice. See [[Institutions substitute state-verification for outcome-inference as ruin exposure rises]].

## Why the ex ante specification is so elaborate

Because liability turns on conduct, the legal labour migrates to specifying conduct in advance: restricted (*muqayyada*) against unrestricted (*muṭlaqa*) *muḍāraba*, the enumeration of permitted goods, routes and markets, the treatment of sales on credit, the prohibition on mixing the capital with the agent's own. Those clauses look like micromanagement and are not. They are **the manufacture of a verifiable conduct standard in a world where outcomes carry no information** — the contract building the observability nature withholds.

## Against the received reading

The standard explanation of the negligence-and-instructions doctrine is that it restores the agent's thin skin in the game, the *muḍārib* risking labour and reputation but not capital. See [[Skin in the game is asymmetric in the mudaraba]]. That reading is not wrong, and it is not the whole account.

Under ruin-dominated dynamics an outcome-based liability rule would convict honest agents at a high rate, because honest failure is a normal feature of the honest trajectory: [[Non-ergodicity collapses the likelihood ratio on outcomes]]. The doctrine is therefore not only compensating for weak incentives; it is **declining an inference the tradition had reason to distrust**. The two readings are complementary, and the second is the one the framework supplies.

## The comparative grid

Every tradition faces the problem and answers it differently, which is what makes this a WP1 strand rather than an Islamic-law aside.

- **Roman law** reaches the same destination by the opposite route: the *receptum nautarum cauponum stabulariorum* imposes strict liability on shipmasters, innkeepers and stablekeepers, then carves out *vis maior* — shipwreck, piracy. The exception does the work the *amāna* default does.
- **The medieval *commenda*** holds the *tractator* harmless absent fault or breach of instructions.
- **General average** requires proof that the sacrifice was deliberate and for the common safety: a conduct fact, not an outcome fact.
- **The sea loan** sits at the opposite pole, forgiving on loss and therefore paying for faked loss. See [[Scuttling rewards fraud in the sea loan]].

The differential prediction follows within a tradition rather than across them: the fault requirement should broaden as ruin exposure rises, so that the standard for a maritime or caravan agent is more forgiving than for a local factor holding goods in a warehouse.

## Verify

- **School differences** are substantial, particularly on the consequences of a violation and on *ʿāriya*. Nothing here should be stated as "Islamic law holds" without specifying the school.
- **The evidentiary rule**, which would be the strongest support available if it holds: my understanding is that a trustee's claim of loss without fault is accepted on his oath — *al-qawl qawl al-amīn bi-yamīnihi* — which would mean the law not only declines the inference but places the burden on the claimant. **Reconstructed, not read. Yaycıoğlu to confirm.**
- **Terminology**: the classical pairing is often *taʿaddī wa-tafrīṭ*, commission and omission, where this vault has been writing *taqṣīr*. Check which the cited fiqh uses.
- Way in: Udovitch (1970) on the *muḍārib*'s liability; Ramli (2018) on al-Sarakhsī's *Kitāb al-Muḍāraba*, the *Mabsūṭ* being the Ḥanafī locus classicus. Both already in the Part 1 bibliography.
