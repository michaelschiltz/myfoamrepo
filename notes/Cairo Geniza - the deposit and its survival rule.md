# Cairo Geniza — the deposit and its survival rule

**The Geniza is not an archive.** It is the storeroom of the Ben Ezra synagogue in Fustat into which the Rabbanite Jews of Old Cairo put written matter they would not destroy, from the early eleventh century to the nineteenth. **About 400,000 fragments** — Rustow's "best count we currently have" — now in some sixty collections. Nobody kept it; it was not arranged; nothing in it was put there to be found.

## The survival rule, and it is partly known

**The received rule** is a deposit norm: writing in Hebrew characters, which might carry the divine name, was not to be destroyed. **Rustow's qualification** is that the norm does not explain behaviour on its own — depositors had different reasons, and paper was dear enough that everyone reused it. Three consequences follow, and all three are selection effects on anything the census reads from it.

**1. It is a script rule.** Judaeo-Arabic, Hebrew and Aramaic in Hebrew characters went in as a matter of course. **Arabic-script documents survive mainly when a Jewish scribe reused the sheet** — wrote on the back or between the lines — and the reused sheet then went in. Rustow's *The Lost Archive* (2020) recovers the Fatimid chancery that way. **The Islamic state's documents are in the Geniza by accident of Jewish stationery.**

**2. It is a period rule.** Rustow distinguishes deposit phases — c.1000–1250, a sixteenth-century phase with Ladino, a nineteenth-century surge. **The "classical Geniza" of Goitein's *Mediterranean Society* is one phase**, and it is the phase every census row touching the Geniza draws on.

**3. It is a community rule.** One synagogue, of one congregation, in one city. **Ackerman-Lieberman's point about positionality is the rule stated as a caution**: documents produced by and for Jews cannot stand unexamined for "Islamic" commercial practice. That is exactly the dispute the census holds open on `isqa_alloc` — see [[Geniza partnership documents - where the census's isqa and qirad evidence sits]].

## The documentary share

**Small, and measured three ways.** Goitein's working count was about 10,000 items of some length, 7,000 of them self-contained documents, half of those near-complete. The Princeton Geniza Project's database now carries **35,855 document records on 36,172 fragments** (February 2026). Against 400,000 fragments, **the documentary Geniza is under a tenth of the deposit.** ⚠️ The three counts use different units and are not reconciled.

## How it left Cairo

**By sale before 1896 and by expedition after.** Dealers and travellers supplied Oxford, St Petersburg (Firkovich, who also bought from the Karaite synagogue's *geniza*), Paris, Budapest and others; Elkan Nathan Adler bought in 1896; **Solomon Schechter removed the greater part in 1896–97** with Charles Taylor's money; Count d'Hulst's excavation sent sixteen sacks to the Bodleian by May 1898. **Pattern 2 inverted**: not successor custody but **acquirer custody** — the community that made the deposit kept none of it. See [[MOC - Acquisitions and the antiquarian trade]].

**The unit that results is the fragment, not the series.** A single document can be split between Cambridge and New York. **The join is the Geniza's equivalent of the European series**, and it is established by palaeography and, now, by computer vision — see [[Friedberg Genizah Project and Ktiv - the image union layer]].
