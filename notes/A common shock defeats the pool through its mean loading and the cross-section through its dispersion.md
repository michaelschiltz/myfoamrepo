# A common shock defeats the pool through its mean loading and the cross-section through its dispersion

The obvious bridge between Objective 1 and Objective 3 — the correlation that limits pooling is the correlation that limits cross-sectional inference — is wrong as stated. The two averaging operations fail on **different moments of the same shock.**

## The two averages

Let member or location *i* receive *b*ᵢ*f*ₜ + εᵢₜ.

- **The pool** averages outcomes: its common component is *f*ₜ·*b̄*, so the shock survives pooling in proportion to the **mean loading**. With loadings of mixed sign the members hedge one another and the shock cancels — the favourable case of [[Pooling's sign is set by the correlation of its baskets]].
- **The cross-sectional regression** is biased through the covariance of regressor and error across units, which scales with the **dispersion of loadings**. Kelly (2026, §3.1) normalises the loadings to mean zero and unit variance; that is the configuration in which inference is most damaged ([[Common shocks leave the cross-section a single realisation]]).

## The claim, if it holds

The configuration that makes pooling work — dispersed loadings, mean near zero, members exposed in offsetting directions — is the one that makes a cross-section least able to stand in for a trajectory. If so, the link between the objectives is a contrast, not a shared mechanism. Where pooling was most valuable, the regional cross-sections the persistence literature reads would carry the most spurious structure. That is a testable statement about the same correlation matrix read two ways.
