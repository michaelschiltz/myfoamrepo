# Japanese archives — custody follows the house

**The structural fact about the Japanese pre-modern record.** In Europe successor custody was the exception the survey counted — five instances. **In Japan it is the default.** The early-modern record was written by households and mostly still sits with them, with their successors, or with whoever received it from them.

## Three kinds of record, three custody paths

**1. The shogunate's own record went to the Meiji state in pieces.** The shogunal libraries and some compiled records became the Naikaku Bunko, now in the National Archives — see [[National Archives of Japan - the Naikaku Bunko and the digital archive]]. The Edo town magistrates' compiled reference records passed through Tokyo Prefecture to the Imperial Library and so to the National Diet Library — [[Kyu-bakufu hikitsugisho - the Edo town magistrate records]]. The Ministry of Finance's records burned in 1923. **There is no national archive of the Tokugawa state**, only what the successor state chose to keep and what survived its fires.

**2. Domains' records stayed with the daimyō houses**, and went later to prefectural archives, universities or family foundations. ⚠️ Not surveyed house by house in this pass.

**3. The village and merchant record — the census's record — was the household's own.** Village headmen's papers (*jikata monjo*) and merchant-house papers (*shōka monjo*) were kept in the *kura* of the family that held the office or ran the business. Where they survive they are **still privately held, or deposited or donated, or sold**:

- **still held by the successor business**: [[Mitsui Bunko - the Mitsui house archive]], [[Sumitomo Historical Archives - Kyoto]]
- **donated to a city**: [[Osaka Museum of History - the Konoike family documents]] — after the 1995 earthquake
- **bought by a university or national body**: [[Shiga University - the Omi merchant archives]]; [[NIJL Archives - the Shiryokan village and merchant fonds]], which bought a headman's papers from a Tokyo second-hand bookseller in 1953
- **found by accident**: [[Toyonaka City - the Chokoji-mura documents]], in the ceiling of a village hall being demolished

## Why this matters to the census specifically

**The *ie* is a coded row, and it is the archive.** `ie` in `organizational_forms` is the household as an entity that outlives its members — [[The ie as entity-shielding]]. **The institution the census codes as a perpetual entity is the institution that kept the records the census is coded from.** A *kō* rulebook survives because a house survived to keep it. **Every Japanese row is therefore a row about survivors in the strongest sense the survey has met**: pattern 7 of [[Handoff - the non-European archive survey]] — failure is where the census is emptiest — has a mechanism here, and the mechanism is custody.

**And it sets the access regime.** Private custody means access by the holder's permission. [[MOC - Digitisation and text recognition]] already records that **permission, not equipment, is the real constraint in Japanese archives**. This note is why.
