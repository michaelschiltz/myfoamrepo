# The qirad's antecedents are Sasanian not Sogdian

Documenting the *ortoq* as transmission (see [[The ortoq is the discriminating instrument]]) relocates the test. The *qirad*'s antecedents lie not east in Sogdiana but in the partnership law of the **Sasanian Near East** — a secular jurisprudence largely lost in its Middle Persian originals and preserved through the East-Syriac law books, the *hambāyīh* standing behind the Ḥanafī and Mālikī *mufāwaḍa* (Morony 2017; cf. Udovitch 1962).

If the Sasanian, Islamic, and Mediterranean forms thus constitute a *single genetically connected western family*, then the genuine test of independent recurrence was never Islamic against Mediterranean — always suspiciously close — but the **East Asian forms against that entire western cluster**, with the *ortoq* as the documented bridge. The comparative payoff (e.g. [[Natie and kabu-nakama are a convergence pair]]) sits precisely on that East-Asia/West seam, so the strand's heaviest evidentiary load falls where the team's archival command is strongest.

## Evidentiary status — unaffirmed

**No consulted expert has yet affirmed the specific genealogy.** De la Vaissière steered the argument *toward* the Sasanian substrate and away from Sogdiana, which is where this note originates. Simonsohn, who works on the East Syriac conduit itself, judges the general proposition plausible — that East Syrian law carried Sasanian legal concepts into the early Islamic legal environment — and separates it explicitly from the case at hand: whether the specific genealogy of the *qirāḍ* can be demonstrated is, in his words, a separate question turning on the strength of the textual evidence. He declines to opine on partnership contracts, and the article he supplied contains no discussion of partnership, commerce or contract at all. See [[Simonsohn 2016 on East Syrian civil law and the Sasanian substrate]].

The claim has now passed through two experts and been handed on twice. That is not refutation, but a Step 2 reviewer with Syriac will see the same pattern, and the design should not depend on it. It does not have to: see [[The western family needs only plausible common descent not proven genealogy]].

## What would settle it, and who

The corpus is locatable. Ishoʿbokht of Rewardashīr's law book — written in Middle Persian, translated into Syriac at the behest of the patriarch Timothy I — is edited in Sachau, *Syrische Rechtsbücher*, vol. 3, 1–201; Kaufhold's survey of the sources maps the rest of the East Syriac corpus.

The method is already demonstrated. Macuch, 'Ein mittelpersischer terminus technicus im syrischen Rechtskodex des Išōʿboḫt und im sasanidischen Rechtsbuch' (1989), traces a Middle Persian technical term present in *both* Ishoʿbokht's Syriac code and the Sasanian law book. That is the *Leitfehler* diagnostic executed for one term. The task is to establish whether a partnership term does the same — a defined philological question rather than a gesture at secondary literature, and therefore something a work package can own.

The people. **Maria Macuch** (FU Berlin, retired 2015; editor and translator of the *Mādayān ī Hazār Dādestān*, author of *Sasanian Law in its Social Context*, whose stated programme is pre-Islamic Iranian law and its impact on Rabbinic, Eastern Christian and Islamic law in formation) is the discipline itself, and the right advisory-board approach. **Hubert Kaufhold** maps the East Syriac corpus. **Lev Weitz** (Catholic University of America) is the recruitable one and has written on the Iranian orientation of Ishoʿbokht's treatise, though his own material is family rather than commercial law.
