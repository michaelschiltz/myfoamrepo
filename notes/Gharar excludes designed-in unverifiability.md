# Gharar excludes designed-in unverifiability

*Gharar* excludes the contracts in which unverifiability is **designed in** — where the payoff turns on a state that cannot be established, so that the form itself pays someone to lie about the state of the world. That is the reading [[Scuttling rewards fraud in the sea loan]] already reaches from the other end: doctrine disfavours a contract whose payoff hangs on an unverifiable event *because it puts a bounty on manufacturing that event*.

Set beside [[The amana-daman structure refuses to infer fault from loss]], the two doctrines resolve into a **single verification programme run in two stages**.

**Stage one, ex ante and at the level of contract selection.** *Gharar* removes from the permitted menu those forms whose payoff structure rewards misrepresentation. The tradition does not attempt to police the lie; it declines to write the contract that makes lying profitable.

**Stage two, ex post and at the level of liability.** Some unverifiability is irreducible — ships do sink, caravans are taken, and no contractual design removes that. The *amāna*/*ḍamān* rule disposes of the residue by refusing to read fault off outcome and shifting the operative test to conduct.

Stated this way the verification reading of *gharar* stops looking like an ad hoc reinterpretation of a doctrine usually explained by metaphysical uncertainty, speculation, or unjust enrichment. It becomes one move in a coherent architecture: **the same problem solved twice, at the two points where it can be attacked.** A tradition that had only *gharar* would still convict honest agents after unverifiable losses; a tradition that had only *amāna*/*ḍamān* would still permit contracts engineered to exploit the refusal. Islamic commercial *fiqh* has both, and they fit.

## Diagnostic value

The reading predicts *which* contracts a tradition should refuse, which makes it testable rather than interpretive. The sea loan is the test case, and it passes twice over: Islamic doctrine refuses it, and — see [[Naviganti - Latin Christendom rejected the sea loan too]] — so does Latin Christendom.

That pairing is worth more than a footnote. Two traditions independently refuse the same contract, and their **stated rationales diverge completely**: Qurʾanic *gharar* and trust-breach reasoning on one side, canon-law usury doctrine and the decretal *Naviganti* on the other. Complex functional agreement carried in wholly divergent arbitrary particulars is exactly the signature described in [[Divergent arbitrary particulars are the independence diagnostic]]. The refusal of the sea loan may therefore be the cleanest independent-recurrence datum the project has — a case where the *thing refused* agrees and every reason given for refusing it differs.

It also tells against the deficit reading of Islamic commercial law. A tradition that removes a contract because its payoff structure rewards fraud is not failing to develop an instrument; it is exercising a design judgement. See [[The deficit reading of absence is the scalar ranking in evidentiary form]] and [[Refusals are observations of the filter not inferences from survivors]].

## Verify

The two-stage reading is ours, not the tradition's own self-description, and should be presented as an analytical reconstruction rather than attributed to the jurists. Whether any classical author states the relation between *gharar* and the *amāna* rule in these terms is unknown to me and worth asking — an affirmative would be a substantial find for the ethnohistoriographical method, since it would put the coding in the actors' own hands.
