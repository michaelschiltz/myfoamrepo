# Brown tests equality where a risk reading predicts dispersion

Brown's most quotable negative finding about *warichi* is that it did not level wealth: "lands are not being redistributed to maintain equality of each participant's landholdings. As with all per share redistribution systems, Iwade's practice was not primarily a wealth-redistributing mechanism." Shares were bought and sold, inequality grew and fluctuated, and Iwade's sixteen shares were fixed by the late seventeenth century without matching the number of families.

**That is a claim about levels, and a risk account makes no prediction about levels.** Insurance operates on dispersion. McCloskey's estimate for scattering is that it cost something like a tenth of mean yield to buy variance reduction — it made peasants poorer *and* safer, which is the whole point of calling it insurance. A redistribution system that leaves the distribution of holdings unequal is therefore entirely compatible with a risk function; the quantity that would test it is the variance of yield per household per year, or the frequency with which a household's holding falls below subsistence, and neither is what Brown measures. The finding is real and it refutes an equality reading that nobody with a risk account needs to hold.

The same inversion applies to *hikikuji*, the right to withhold land from redistribution. Brown reads its concentration as a defect — exemptions ran from under 2 per cent of village land in 1756–57 to 14 per cent by 1818, exceeded the 5 per cent Kaga permitted, and "the rights to withhold were concentrated in a few hands, not universally shared," which "departs significantly from standard understandings of how warichi operated." Under a risk reading that is not a departure but a prediction: **those best able to bear variance withdraw from the variance-sharing device**, and the growth of exemptions through the late eighteenth century tracks rising capacity to self-insure. It is the mechanism McCloskey uses to explain enclosure — open fields die when cheaper insurance appears — observed inside an institution rather than at its dissolution.

Which places *warichi* alongside the other cases in this project where a formally equalising arrangement carries an internal channel favouring the strong ([[The tontine is pooling in form and dispersal in function]], [[The bidding ko transfers from the liquidity-constrained to the liquid]]). The difference is instructive: in the tontine and the bidding kō the channel is constitutive of the mechanism, whereas *hikikuji* is an opt-out from it. A device you can leave and a device that redistributes toward you while you stay are not the same object, and the census should not collapse them.

The larger point is about what Brown's apparatus can and cannot reach. The 2011 book runs on property rights and commons governance — Barzel, Libecap, North, Ostrom, Hardin — yet by his own account every candidate explanation in that family fails to separate villages with *warichi* from villages without, leaving natural hazard as "the only distinguishing characteristic." The descriptive frame and the explanatory variable come from different theories, and the evidence assembled to test the second is scored against expectations belonging to the first.

**Do not mistake who holds the risk thesis.** Spreading the impact of flooding is the received reading in the Japanese literature, resting on individual village studies; the 2006 article is Brown's attempt to falsify it at regional scale, and it contains the phrases "property right" and "insurance" not once. His verdict is that hazard frequency is an insufficient cause "even if scholars ultimately see [it] as [a] necessary condition." Arguing that *warichi* is more risk-driven than Brown allows is therefore siding with the orthodoxy against its only regional test — a weak position to occupy by accident. The defensible move is narrower: his test scores hazard as *exposure*, and the account that survives it predicts from *persistence* ([[Warichi re-randomises after absorption rather than diversifying]]).

Two things not to overclaim. Brown may yet be right that hazard does not explain *warichi* — his own GIS mapping shows the practice well above the floodplains and not confined to lowlands, and the concluding verdict of his ch. 6 has not been read here. And the reinterpretation of *hikikuji* offered above is a hypothesis with an obvious test: exemption-holders should be the larger shareholders, and Brown's tables record both. Until that is run it is a reading, not a result.
