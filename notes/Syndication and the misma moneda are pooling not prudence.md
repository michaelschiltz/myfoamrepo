# Syndication and the misma moneda are pooling not prudence

Three structures in Philip II's credit system are described by Drelichman and Voth as prudence, and all three are pooling.

Syndication first. Of 438 transactions, 141 had more than one lender, accounting for 30 per cent of all money lent (2014: 146), and the reason the book gives is that "no prudent banker would hold that much risk in his own portfolio" (11).

The retail pass-through second. Savers bought a share in a banker's loan rather than a deposit with him, and were repaid *en la misma moneda*, in the same currency: losses reached them in proportion to their stake instead of being absorbed by a levered intermediary (3).

The absence of leverage third, which the authors identify without naming its consequence. "The Spanish system avoided the risks of leverage. Bankers did not end up holding the most 'toxic' portion of assets, as modern-day banks did in the 2000s" (3).

Prudence is the wrong category, because it makes all three preferences. Pooling raises the time-average growth rate of every participant while leaving every participant's expectation unchanged, so it is chosen by anyone optimising a trajectory and by nobody optimising a mean. Leverage is the mirror operation: it raises the ensemble average and lowers the time average past an optimum. The sixteenth-century pass-through and the collateralised debt obligation were built for the same stated purpose and differ on exactly this axis. Their ensemble averages were comparable; their time averages were not, and that is the difference the authors are pointing at when they draw the 2007 contrast.

## The correlation qualification bites here

[[Pooling's sign is set by the correlation of its baskets]] applies and is not a formality. Overlapping Genoese syndicates pooled exposures to a single borrower, so the baskets were highly correlated and the growth-rate gain from syndication taken alone was modest. Syndication's real work was the network of multilateral obligations that made the moratoria hold, which is an enforcement property, not a pooling one. The pooling gain came from the pass-through, which spread the king's shortfalls across a much wider and much less correlated body of savers.

Separating the two is the contribution: the book treats syndication and pass-through as one risk-transfer story, and they are two mechanisms doing different work.
