# Clark's institution-invariant law leaves heredity as the only carrier

Clark's stationarity fights on two fronts at once. Against estimates of fast mobility — parent–child elasticities around 0.3–0.4, "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations" — it argues for slow regression, a latent persistence near 0.7–0.75 per generation. Against permanent class, caste, or the compounding of inherited capital in Piketty's sense, it argues that regression is eventually complete. What the two fronts share is that institutions do not set the rate: *The Son Also Rises* presents mobility rates as alike across societies and resistant to social policy.

Once the law is held fixed across regimes and invariant to institutions, the persistent component cannot be lodged in anything those regimes rebuild. Clark and Cummins (2026) take the step explicitly: random shocks to family size altered children's wealth in England before 1880 but left no trace three generations on, while wealth itself persisted beyond five; they conclude that persistence runs through the familial inheritance of behaviours associated with accumulation and retention, not through wealth transfers. The 2023 PNAS paper carries the same structure towards a form that parallels additive genetic determination under assortative mating. The hereditarian reading is not bolted onto the stationary model; a stationary, time-homogeneous model leaves room for nothing else. That, more than any dispute over method, is what draws the hostility.

The note complements [[A persistence parameter stable across regime change indicts the measure]], which argues that the invariance implicates the surname apparatus. The claim here is prior: the invariance is what forces the conclusion, so the conclusion is only as strong as the case for time-homogeneity. Clark's disclaimers on genetic causation stand, as that note records; vocabulary as fixed in [[Never let the units slide from instruments to populations]].
