# The moratorium held because leaving the pool is not a one-period cost

In 1575 and again in 1596 the Genoese lenders cut Philip II off as a body. No member broke rank, no side deal was cut, and the settlements were accordingly known as *medio general*. Drelichman and Voth call the fact startling, given that the royal camp made repeated and generous offers, and explain it by a cheat-the-cheater mechanism: whoever lent during the moratorium would be defaulted on in turn, and the Fugger correspondence shows the incentive was understood (2014: 6, 134).

The mechanism is well evidenced and this note does not dispute it. It is carrying more weight than it needs to.

A side deal is a gain in expectation available in one period, set against exit from the arrangement that generates the participant's growth rate in every subsequent period. Where the arrangement is what makes the trajectory grow, the comparison is not between two payoffs but between a payoff and a rate, and it does not turn on whether off-path retaliation is credible. Refusal becomes a dominance result rather than an equilibrium requiring correctly anticipated punishment.

This lowers what the enforcement story has to carry, and it accounts for a residual the enforcement story does not. The moratoria held twice, twenty-one years apart, with partly different personnel and under offers the authors describe as generous. Equilibria that depend on every participant correctly forecasting a punishment are fragile to exactly that turnover. Growth-rate comparisons are not, because each participant can reach the conclusion alone, from his own books, without modelling anyone else's reasoning.

## Relation to the lending data

The prediction is that participants behave as though the relationship, not the contract, is the asset. That is what the turnover figures show: 85.4 per cent of borrowing came from bankers who had lent recently, a banker who lent once had an 88 per cent chance of lending again, and exit rates barely move across the 1575 break, from 3.8 to 4.4 per cent (2014: 149). See [[Time-average optimization is a survivorship property not an intention]] for why this is evidence about selection rather than about intention.
