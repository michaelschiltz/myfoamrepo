# The comanda ad societatem shares losses in the proportion of profits

Some Catalan comandas split capital losses between investor and agent in the same proportion as profits, so the agent bears part of the principal's loss. The unilateral comanda never makes him do that ([[The Catalan maritime comanda puts the whole capital loss on the investor]]). The acts call these contracts *comanda ad societatem*, *comanda sive societas* or simply *societas*. The difference is a loss rule, and it cuts across sea and land.

**By land, in a craft.** In a Vic act of 1273, two shoemakers take 100 s. "in pura comanda ad societatem" for wine and their craft. They are to halve the profit after the capital is deducted, and "si diminutio in dicto capitali venerit mitamus nos et tu talem partem qualem accepimus in lucro", for one year, *quisque in solidum* (García Sanz, *Ausa* 1959, 292–293, doc. 5; [[Garcia Sanz on the Vic commendas of the thirteenth century]]). Martínez Gijón finds the same rule in 1261 acts with butchers and shoemakers taking money *in comanda ad societatem*. There the agents take two thirds or a half of the profit "e idéntica proporción para las pérdidas" ([[Martinez Gijon on the comenda in Spanish law]], 1966, 387–388). At Manresa in 1323 a skinner and his wife take 100 s. "in facto mercaterie ad terciam partem lucre et amissiones" (Fynn-Paul 2017, 95 n. 31). Fynn-Paul's translation keeps only the profit.

**By sea.** Vic *societates* of 1230 and 1231 have unilateral capital, carried to Mallorca or "in terra vel in mari". They halve profits and losses alike, "si forte … diminutio ibi venerit restituam tibi medium et tu restituas tibi aliam medietatem" (Martínez Gijón 1966, 404–405). The 1239 *comanda sive societas* halves profit and risk although the agent contributed only 60 s. against 260 s. (386, 388).

**What it is.** Martínez Gijón reads the craft acts as bilateral, the agent's trade counting as his contribution. He holds that they "do not differ substantially" from the maritime bilateral comanda: "ni siquiera en las bilaterales la participación en los riesgos se configura de modo diferente" (387). He also reads the 1230–1231 *societates* as comandas, not partnerships, because only the agent gives guarantees (405–406). The rule is the *societas* rule, *per solidum atque libram*. Within the commenda family this is the position of the bilateral form; compare [[Unilateral and bilateral commenda collapse on profit and separate on loss]].

**How strong.** The acts are few and mostly thirteenth-century: Vic 1230, 1231, 1239, 1273; Barcelona 1261; Manresa 1323. They come from editions by García Sanz, Carreras y Candi and Sayous, and from one secondary author's reading. Martínez Gijón stresses that the unilateral form "predominates in Spanish law" and that bilateral documents are "escasos" (386). Fynn-Paul did not tally the loss clause across his 91 Manresa commendas.
