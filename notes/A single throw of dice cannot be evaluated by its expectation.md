# A single throw of dice cannot be evaluated by its expectation

Drelichman and Voth defend the Armada as an ex ante reasonable venture. Contemporaries including Raleigh took it seriously, Spain had recently destroyed the Ottoman fleet at Lepanto, and the realised outcome "must have been toward the most pessimistic end of the range of possible outcomes" (2014: 126–27). They then separate it from the Netherlands campaign: "The Armada was a single throw of dice; the Dutch War represented a continuous, expensive, and seemingly interminable commitment" (127).

The distinction is real and the inference drawn from it runs backwards.

An expectation is an average across the branches of a throw. It is the right criterion when the throw is repeated and the stake is replenished between throws, because the time average of the realised sequence then converges on it. Neither condition holds for the Armada. Philip threw once, and the stake did not survive the throw: a third of the fleet and half the lives went with it, and those ships did not finance the next attempt. Under multiplicative dynamics a venture can carry a positive expectation and a negative time-average growth rate at once, and the gap between the two widens with the fraction of the stock exposed. The Armada exposed a large fraction.

So the authors' own contrast supports the opposite reading. It is the *continuous* commitment in the Netherlands, where exposure per period was smaller and the sequence long, that comes closest to satisfying the conditions under which an expectation is the correct criterion. The single throw comes furthest from them. Their intuition that the two cases differ is right; the ordering they impose on that difference is an artefact of evaluating both by the same mean.

Nothing here requires the decision to have been unreasonable by contemporary lights, and nothing here requires Philip's advisers to have reasoned in growth rates. It requires only that "reasonable ex ante" be evaluated against the growth rate of the trajectory rather than the mean of its branches, and the two criteria disagree precisely on gambles of this shape.

## Consequence

The book's Ecclesiastes gloss ("time and chance happeneth to them all") is doing work it cannot do. Time and chance happening to all members of an ensemble is compatible with almost every member declining.
