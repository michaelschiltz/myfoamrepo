# The opgeld prices the peril without separating it from interest

Thirty-six Amsterdam *contracten van bodemerij* between 1601 and 1621 fix the cost of
carrying the sea peril before the ship sails, and attach it to the peril in words: *tot
opgelt ende voor 't pericule ende aventuyr van der zee*. The figure is not a convention. It
moves with the voyage — 5⅛ per cent Königsberg to Amsterdam, 7 to 12 for a single Baltic
run, 24 to 35 where an Iberian leg is added, and Winkelman reports 70 for a Brazil
destination elsewhere in the same series. Six acts carry a **schedule** of alternative rates
inside one instrument, keyed to which route the master takes: 34 or 27, 35 or 25, 31 or 21,
10 or 18, 21 or 27, 17/17/9. Where the route falls outside the contract altogether the rate
goes to arbitrators, *tot seggen ende discretie van goede luyden hen dies verstaende*.

That is ex ante pricing of a peril, articulated by the parties, in a northern port a
generation before the actuarial literature.

**What is not articulated is the decomposition.** The opgeld is one number. No act
separates a return on the money lent from a charge for bearing the sea. Winkelman separates
them in his Inleiding — *het is rente voor de lening en een premie voor het risico* — and
Verwer, whom he quotes, goes further and computes what the creditor keeps *na aftrek van
desen voorraed tot Asseurantie*, the deduction being what reinsurance would cost. Both are
reasoning from outside the instrument, and Verwer has a motive: the residue must be shown
to fall inside the usury maximum.

So the coding takes the priced peril as articulated and refuses the split. **Any interest
rate or any pure risk premium recovered from these figures is analyst-imposed, and a
codebook that records it otherwise has laundered an inference into a reading.** The
practical test is the one the sources themselves fail: two contracts on the same day at
different rates tell you the peril was priced, and tell you nothing about where the price
divides.
