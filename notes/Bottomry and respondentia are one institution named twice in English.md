# Bottomry and respondentia are one institution named twice in English

The pair looks like a distinction between institutions. It is a distinction inside one institution,
drawn by one legal language and generalised by nineteenth-century systematisers who wrote in it.

Schuster gives the etymology and the map in a single footnote:

> Der Name Bodmerei kommt von *bodeme* (= Schiffsboden) als **Gesamtbezeichnung von Schiff und
> Ladung**; erstmals verwendet wird er im Amsterdamer Statut von 1527 („bomerie"). **Im Englischen
> bottomry (bei Verpfändung des Schiffes) bzw. respondentia (falls nur die Ladung verpfändet
> werden) genannt**, niederländisch bodemerij, dänisch / schwedisch bodmeri. Im Französischen …
> *contrat à la grosse*, im Italienischen … *prestito a cambio marittimo*, im Spanischen …
> *préstamo a la gruesa ventura*.

The root term **covers ship and cargo together**. Dutch, Danish, Swedish, French, Italian and Spanish
all name the institution without subdividing it. English alone splits it by pledge object, and the
split is nominal: the two names pick out which parameter was filled, not two contracts with
different structures.

## The English evidence is itself lopsided

Steckley's sample of the High Court of Admiralty, 1608–1699, yields **58 bottomry cases**. The word
*respondentia* occurs once in his article, in a footnote citing a modern textbook chapter. If the
pair were a working distinction in the jurisdiction that coined it, the cargo half would appear in
the litigation.

The functional story fails on the same evidence. In 1678 Captain Cole pledged "the keel or bottom"
of the *Golden Phoenix* **to buy a cargo of Portuguese salt** — ship-secured, cargo-financing. Hoover
had already made the general point for Genoa: it was not necessary that the borrowed funds be used
in connection with the property pledged.

## The route by which the pair became canonical

Zanini traces the tidy dichotomy to where it actually lives — Baldasseroni's *Dizionario ragionato*
(1810) and Annesley's *Compendium* (1824) — and notes that those same authorities also recognise
**a mixed form of the two**. A nineteenth-century treatise-writer needed exhaustive categories;
the notaries did not.

## Why it matters beyond the name

Two rows differing in one cell, and inheriting the rest from a shared parent, are not two
observations. They are one observation and a parameter setting. The point is familiar from a quite
different domain — see [[Forms are not independent observations when a village runs a portfolio]] —
and it has the same consequence here: the diversity budget is denominated in independent forms, and
a nominal subdivision does not buy one.

Keeping the rows is defensible; what is not defensible is counting them twice. The honest record is
that `bottomry` stands on a contemporary legal form and `respondentia` stands on an analyst's split
following nineteenth-century English usage — which is exactly the distinction the `boundary_basis`
column exists to carry, and exactly why it was declared before this evidence arrived.
