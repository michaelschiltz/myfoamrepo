# The hive models the corporation's emergent layer not the route by which it formed

**The corporation does what the hive does: a layer forms above the members, sets a horizon longer than any of theirs, and lays down rules for how they deal with one another and with outsiders; the members accept limits on their own conduct because they gain from the arrangement. As an image of the emergent layer, the hive is right. As an image of the route, it is not. Boomsma and Gawne 2018 argue that every insect superorganism arose top-down, from a family founded by lifetime monogamy, and never by unrelated individuals coming together. Put unrelated bees together and you do not get a hive; they fight. The corporation's route runs through unrelated members whose interests are aligned by mutual gain, not by kinship, and its boundary is held by law, not by the members' biology. And it differs from the hive on exit: the capital is locked in, the member is not.** *(Revised 2026-11-13 after MS's objection; the first version of this note, titled "The hive is the wrong model for pools of unrelated partners", ran together the outcome and the route.)*

## The outcome: an emergent layer with its own horizon and rules

What makes the hive the right image is not the comb or the store but the layer: a unit whose horizon outruns its members' lives and whose rules govern their interactions. Perpetual succession is the corporation's version of the first ([[Pooling approaches the infinite ensemble and perpetual succession the infinite horizon]]); its constitution and by-laws are the second. The horizon point also ties to [[Fitness decoupling may be a short-horizon artefact]]: on the unit's horizon the members' and the unit's interests coincide, which is the reading of *Meditations* 6.54 the project wants. The members' acceptance of limits is not altruism. It is the gain the time-average argument predicts for those who pool within a closed unit ([[Pooling raises the rate and individuation draws the boundary]]).

## The route: kinship in the hive, mutual gain among strangers

Boomsma and Gawne (16–19, read in full) set three steps: founders sequestered as specialised breeders; some offspring committed to somatic roles; and a lifelong mated/unmated caste split, the "point of no return." Lifetime monogamy is the necessary condition because it makes relatedness to offspring and to siblings exactly equal. Polyandry, policing and queen adoption came afterwards. "Bottom-up coming together completely lacks the prospect of ever realizing a major fraternal evolutionary transition" (19). MS's gloss puts it concretely: a heap of unrelated bees is not a hive, because without the alignment kinship supplies, and without a shared colony odour, they treat one another as intruders. Beekeepers merging two colonies separate them with a sheet of newspaper so that the odours mix while the bees chew through, which is a small demonstration that the boundary has to be negotiated before the unit exists.

The transition literature also knows the other route: organismality reached by unlike, unrelated partners, of which the eukaryotic cell is the classic case, and which Queller and Strassmann 2009 extend to mutualisms such as figs and their pollinators. They list "clonality or high relatedness" among the traits *not* essential to organismality; Boomsma and Gawne reject their continuum as lumping. The corporation belongs to this route, and the question it poses is the one kinship answers for the hive: what aligns the partners? The time-average gain within a closed pool is the project's answer.

## Two differences the analogy must keep

- **Exit.** In the hive the point of no return is the worker's irreversible loss of reproductive autonomy; she cannot leave and cannot breed on her own account. In the corporation capital lock-in is the counterpart, but it binds the capital, not the person: the share can be sold. The unit acquires permanence while the members keep a way out. The hive has neither, and this decoupling of the unit's permanence from the members' commitment is the corporation's own architecture ([[Arthur's lock-in and capital lock-in are different objects]]).
- **The boundary's source.** The hive holds its boundary by colony odour and guards. The corporation holds its boundary against members' creditors only through property law imposed on third parties (Hansmann, Kraakman and Squire 2006; [[Legal scaffolding gives a pool collective properties it does not yet own]]). Strangers who have no kinship to align them and no shared odour to recognise one another need an outside rule to make them count as one.

## Where the household fits

The *ie* is the institutional case closest to the hive's route: a household founded on a lifetime commitment, perpetuated by succession and adoption, with members whose roles are fixed for life ([[The ie as entity-shielding]], [[The fictive household supplied partition and perpetuity without legal personality]]). The comparison between the *ie* and the corporation is then a comparison between the two routes to the same kind of layer.
