# Amsterdam notaries drafted ship-pledged and goods-pledged bodemerij as one instrument

The English-language literature divides the secured sea loan by what is pledged: the hull
takes one name, the cargo another. Amsterdam practice around 1600 does not make the cut.

Two notaries, Jan Franssen Bruyningh and Jacob Meerhout, drew all thirty-six surviving
contracts. Their instruments are built from the same parts in the same order —
acknowledgement of receipt, the money declared to run *op 't recht aventuyr van der zee*,
the itinerary closing on *wel heynder maer niet verder te seylen*, the repayment interval
in days after arrival, the opgeld, and the *verbindende* clause. **Only the noun in the
last clause changes.** One act pledges the keel and gear, the next the salt and its
returns, another the freight first and the hull second, another a fifth of a hull, another
the borrower's whole estate present and future. Two acts of the same day, same lender, same
borrower, differ in nothing: the second is entered simply as *in aller vougen ende manieren
als vooren verhaelt is*.

Every one of them is headed *contract van bodemerij*, and one invokes a customary law for
the whole class by name — money running *naer recht ende costume van bodemerije*. **No
Dutch term in the corpus corresponds to the cargo-pledged variant at all.**

The claim this supports is narrow and it is about our categories rather than theirs. A
distinction that organises the modern secondary literature, and that entered this project's
typology as an analyst's split, has no purchase on the drafting practice of the port that
was doing most of the business. **It is a comparative concept, not a descriptive category**
in Haspelmath's sense, and the row that carries it should keep saying so.

What the pledge object does mark is a difference in **who borrows**. The hull acts are the
master's or the part-owner's, taken *tot behoeve ende nootelijcke uutredinge* — for the
necessary fitting-out. The cargo acts are the merchant's, on his own goods and their
returns, and they are the ones that reach for the language of insurance. That is a
difference between two uses of one instrument, and it is not what the received distinction
names.
