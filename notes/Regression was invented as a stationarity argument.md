# Regression was invented as a stationarity argument

Not to be confused with [[Galton's problem]], Galton's other question — the independence of cross-cultural cases, put to Tylor in 1889. This note concerns the tool, not the comparison.

Galton's question in "Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature" (1886) was why the population distribution of height stays the same from one generation to the next although height is inherited: heredity alone should widen it. Reversion towards the mean was the reconciliation. The regression coefficient was therefore born as the parameter that keeps a population distribution stationary across generations, and mobility research inherits the premise with the name. Champernowne (1953) derives the Pareto law as the stationary distribution of a Markov chain on income classes; Becker and Tomes (1979) call theirs "An Equilibrium Theory of the Distribution of Income and Intergenerational Mobility".

Galton also saw that the relation runs both ways: under a stationary distribution parents regress on children as children on parents [verify locus — 1886 paper or *Natural Inheritance*, 1889]. The symmetry is the subject of [[A stationary Gaussian AR(1) has no arrow of time]].

Quah's "Galton's fallacy" (1993) turns the premise against convergence regressions: coefficients of either sign are consistent with an unchanging cross-section distribution. Clark's surname apparatus — a latent status following a first-order autoregression with a constant coefficient — is Galton's, with the stationarity premise intact.
