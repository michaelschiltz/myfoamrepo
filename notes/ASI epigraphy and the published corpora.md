# ASI epigraphy and the published corpora

**The Archaeological Survey of India's Epigraphy Branch at Mysuru is the custodian of India's inscriptional record** — as **estampages**, ink impressions taken in the field, which are the working surrogate for inscriptions that stay where they are. ⚠️ The size of the estampage collection was not established in this pass.

## The layers

- **Layer 1**: the ***Annual Report on Indian Epigraphy***, which lists the inscriptions copied each year. Volumes from the 1920s onward are scanned on the Internet Archive; a private site republishes some years.
- **Layer 3**: ***Epigraphia Indica*** and ***South Indian Inscriptions*** — edited texts with translations, published from the 1890s. **Old enough to be out of copyright and online.**
- **Layer 2**: the estampages themselves. ⚠️ Digitisation status not established.
- **Machine-readable**: partial, and outside the ASI — see [[DHARMA - an ERC Synergy project that produced a TEI corpus of inscriptions]]. The **Digital Archive of South Indian Inscriptions**, an Uppsala–Cologne project begun in 2002 with SGML encoding, is the earlier attempt; ⚠️ its current status was not established and the report read is the project's own first report.

## The honest assessment

**Important and awkward.** The corpus that carries [[Indian inscriptions - the endowment recorded in stone]] is published and free — **but it is a century of philological editing organised by dynasty, language and findspot, not by institution**, and there is no index of endowments, rates or trustees. **Finding the census's cases means reading the corpora or the secondary literature that has already read them**, which is how the Chinese pass had to work too.
