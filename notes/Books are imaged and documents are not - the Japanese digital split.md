# Books are imaged and documents are not — the Japanese digital split

**Japan is very widely served digitally — for books.** The expectation holds, and it holds unevenly, and the unevenness runs straight through the census's evidence.

## The served half: *kotenseki* and the state's records

- **[[Japan Search - the national metadata aggregator]]**: 32.8 million records from 320 databases and 208 organisations.
- **[[NIJL Kokusho Database - the classical books union layer]]**: original images of 300,000 items targeted by the end of FY2024, IIIF, NIJL's own images public domain.
- **[[National Diet Library - Digital Collections and the kotenseki OCR layer]]**: 4.23 million items full-text searchable; **75,000 *kotenseki* OCR'd on 1 September 2026** with NDL古典籍OCR ver. 3.
- **[[National Archives of Japan - the Naikaku Bunko and the digital archive]]**: the shogunal libraries, catalogued online, key manuscripts imaged — including the medieval diary the census's earliest *kō* row is read from.
- **[[Minna de Honkoku - crowdsourced transcription]]**: 48 million characters transcribed by more than 10,800 volunteers.

## The unserved half: *komonjo* in local and private custody

**The documents the census needs — *kō* rulebooks, subscription ledgers, pledge deeds, village accounts — are archival documents, not books.** They sit in the custody described at [[Japanese archives - custody follows the house]], and there the digital layer thins to a catalogue or to nothing:

- The NIJL's own Echigo village fonds: "images not online", "database search not yet possible" — see [[Echigo - the NIJL fonds behind the census's Echigo rows]].
- Toyonaka's 4,619 Chōkōji-mura documents, the source of three census rows: a designated municipal cultural property with no digital publication found.
- The Mitsui and Sumitomo archives: appointment only, originals shown as reproductions.

**Rule: an institution's being in Japan Search says nothing about whether its *komonjo* are imaged.** Check the fonds, not the portal.

## Why the split exists, and it is the funding map again

**Books were digitised because they are catalogued, uniform, owned by the bodies that digitised them, and demanded by literary scholarship.** Documents are none of those. The NIJL's own programme is named for *kotenseki*, the classical texts; the OCR models were trained on printed and manuscript books. **The survey's first pattern — the coverage map is a legibility map — reappears as a genre map**: Japanese digitisation followed literature, not economic history.

## The one exception that matters

**Japan's documents *are* widely available at layer 3 — in print.** See [[Jichitaishi - the municipal histories as Japan's print layer 3]]. Every Japanese row the census carries was coded from a printed transcription or from a historian quoting one.
