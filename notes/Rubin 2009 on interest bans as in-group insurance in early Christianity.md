# Rubin 2009 on interest bans as in-group insurance in early Christianity

Jared Rubin, "Social Insurance, Commitment, and the Origin of Law: Interest Bans in Early Christianity," *Journal of Law and Economics* 52:4 (2009), 761–786. DOI 10.1086/595796. SSRN 1003288 is the open version.

**An origin story in which the ban serves the group, paired in Rubin's own later work with a persistence story in which it serves rulers.** The pairing is the reason to hold the paper.

**The argument** [verify against the text]. The ban originated in the early Church as a commitment device supporting mutual insurance among co-religionists. A small, exposed community that forbade itself to profit from members in distress made its mutual aid credible. This is Glaeser and Scheinkman's insurance logic ([[Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998 on interest restrictions as social insurance]]) given a group and a commitment problem.

**The companion that matters more.** Rubin's later papers — "Institutions, the Rise of Commerce and the Persistence of Laws" (*Economic Journal* 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2011.02456.x), "The Lender's Curse" (*Journal of Economic History* 2008), and "Bills of Exchange, Interest Bans, and Impersonal Exchange" (*Explorations in Economic History* 2010) — explain the bans' **persistence** in Islam and their erosion in Christian Europe by the rulers' dependence on religious legitimacy. The function that produced the rule and the filter that kept it are different things, in one author's work. That is the strongest available illustration of [[Taleb reads survival as evidence about the survivor and we read it as evidence about the filter]], and the project should cite it as such.

**Where the project departs.** As with Glaeser and Scheinkman, the insurance is in expected utility. The barrier version would ask whether the community's members were kept off debt bondage, and whether the community itself outlasted comparable groups without the rule. Neither is measured.
