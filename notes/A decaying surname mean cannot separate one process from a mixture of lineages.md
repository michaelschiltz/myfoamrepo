# A decaying surname mean cannot separate one process from a mixture of lineages

Clark's evidence is the decay of surname-group means towards the population mean. A group mean is an ensemble average over lineages. Its decay at a constant rate is what a single first-order autoregression applied to every lineage predicts; over any finite horizon it is also what a mixture predicts — most lineages mixing fast, a minority held near their origin or absorbed at the top — provided the minority shrinks at the right rate. The two diverge only in the limit, where the mixture leaves the group mean above the population mean for good.

This is Heckman's (1991) problem of separating heterogeneity from state dependence, restated for lineages, and the same defect recorded for the anti-Semitism series at [[Voigtlander and Voth measured the relaxation time and reported a mixture]]. Blume, Cholli, Durlauf and Lukina (2024) supply a Markov-chain instrument for it: "memory curves" by class of origin, which show the memory of origin dissipating within about three generations on average, with real heterogeneity across dynasties of different origin. The distribution of lineage-level decay rates, not the group mean, is the object an ergodicity criterion needs.
