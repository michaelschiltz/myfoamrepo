# Scott 1976 on the subsistence ethic as safety-first

James C. Scott, *The Moral Economy of the Peasant: Rebellion and Subsistence in Southeast Asia* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1976). Formal ancestor: A. D. Roy, "Safety First and the Holding of Assets," *Econometrica* 20 (1952) [not in library; acquire]. Applied to subsistence farming by Kunreuther and Wright, "Safety-First, Gambling, and the Subsistence Farmer" (1974) [venue unverified].

**The closest existing statement of "ethics as the gateway to survival", and its central comparison is the vault's outcome-coupling.**

**The argument** [from the standard reading of the book; verify page loci].

- **The subsistence ethic.** Cultivators near the margin minimise the probability of disaster rather than maximising average return.
- **The moral economy.** The norms of the village — a right to subsistence, reciprocity, obligations on patrons in bad years — encode that preference.
- **Legitimacy is judged by what is left, not by what is taken.** A share rent that falls with the harvest is tolerable. A fixed rent, or a colonial head tax due in cash whatever the crop, is experienced as exploitation even when it is lower on average.
- **Rebellion follows.** Scott traces the rebellions of 1930–31 (Nghe-Tinh; Saya San) to fixed claims meeting bad years.

**Why it matters.**

- **The comparison is outcome-coupling, read as morality.** The peasant prefers the claim whose size moves with the realised outcome, because the fixed claim carries the barrier. That is the second binding of [[Lock-in and outcome-coupling are distinct bindings]].
- **The doctrinal refusals say the same thing.** The refusal of the priced-certain return in [[Ribbit mandates exposure rather than forbidding gain]] and [[Islamic doctrine refuses risk-commodification at step one]] is that moral judgement written as doctrine.
- **Safety-first is a criterion on the trajectory, not on the ensemble.** Read with [[Time-average]], it is the time-average criterion stated by people who lived it, without the formalism.

**The objection to pre-empt.** Popkin, *The Rational Peasant* (1979), answers Scott with individual calculation [not in library; acquire]. Fafchamps 1992 (*Economic Development and Cultural Change*, DOI 10.1086/452001) reconciles the two — rational peasants with a moral economy — and is the safer citation for the mechanism. Henrich has argued that peasants are risk-prone, not risk-averse (1999). That reverses the preference and cuts against reading safety-first as risk aversion, which is the reading the project also rejects.
