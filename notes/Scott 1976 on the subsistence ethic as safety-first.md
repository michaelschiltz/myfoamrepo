# Scott 1976 on the subsistence ethic as safety-first

James C. Scott, *The Moral Economy of the Peasant: Rebellion and Subsistence in Southeast Asia* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1976). Introduction and chapter 1 read in the scanned PDF (OCR), October 2026; page numbers are the book's.

**The closest existing statement of "ethics as the gateway to survival". Its central figure is an ensemble-against-trajectory comparison, drawn by hand: the claim that takes most on average is the one peasants resent least.**

## The argument, verified

- **The dilemma (p. 4).** The peasant household lives "close to the subsistence margin" and "seeks to avoid the failure that will ruin him rather than attempting a big, but risky, killing."
- **Safety-first (pp. 17–18).** The cultivator "prefers to minimize the probability of having a disaster rather than maximizing his average return." Scott takes the term from Roumasset 1971, a Wisconsin working paper on rice in the Philippines (n. 8), not directly from Roy 1952.
- **The techniques (p. 5).** The principle "lies behind a great many of the technical, social, and moral arrangements" of the precapitalist village. Scattered strips are the first example, "often at the cost of a reduction in average" yield. Periodically redistributed communal land and the commons are the social ones.
- **The criterion of exploitation (pp. 29–31).**
  - "The peasant's criterion will be what is left after outside claims have been met … rather than the level of the claims per se" (p. 29).
  - Figure 2 (p. 30) sets a fixed claim (B) against a variable claim (C) that "steadies the net resources left for the peasant household."
  - "The key element for the peasant in judging the exactions … is whether they increase or reduce the chance of a disaster" (p. 31).
  - Claim C takes more in total. "If we were to use as a standard of exploitation the average 'take' of elites from peasants, then the stabilizing claim would qualify as the most exploitative." Yet it "is less resented, stirs less ferocious resistance" (p. 31).
- **The state as landlord (p. 8).** Colonial fiscal advisers preferred "fixed head taxes and fixed land rates" to a tax on actual income. When the Depression cut variable revenues, the state "bore down more heavily on its most steady revenue producer, the head tax."
- **Rebellion.** The Saya San Rebellion in Burma and the Nghe-Tinh Soviets in Vietnam (1930–31) are the case studies (pp. 4, 7).

## Why it matters

- **Page 31 is the ensemble/trajectory split, stated by a political scientist.**
  - The "average take" is the ensemble measure of a claim.
  - "Whether they increase or reduce the chance of a disaster" is the trajectory measure.
  - Scott shows that the two rank the same claims in opposite order, and that peasants use the second. That is the vault's [[The split is over whether the explanandum is a state or a trajectory]], with a moral sentiment as the observable.
- **The comparison is outcome-coupling, read as morality.** The tolerable claim is the one whose size moves with the realised outcome; the fixed claim carries the barrier. That is the second binding of [[Lock-in and outcome-coupling are distinct bindings]]. Hudson's agrarian/commercial sort is the same line ([[Hudson 2002 on clean slates as protection of the free population]]).
- **The doctrinal refusals say the same thing.** The refusal of the priced-certain return in [[Ribbit mandates exposure rather than forbidding gain]] and [[Islamic doctrine refuses risk-commodification at step one]] is that moral judgement written as doctrine.
- **Scattered strips connect to the ergodicity literature.** Peters and Adamou cite land scattering as cooperation that raises time-average growth. Scott lists it as the first safety-first technique, at a cost in average yield. One practice, two descriptions: a loss on the ensemble and a gain on the trajectory.

## What Scott does not claim

**He uses the vocabulary of risk aversion.** "In decision-making parlance his behavior is risk-averse; he minimizes the subjective probability of the maximum loss" (p. 4). The time-average reading is therefore the project's redescription, not Scott's. What the project can claim is that his *criterion* (the chance of disaster on one household's path) is a trajectory criterion, whatever he called it.

**Rebellion is not survival.** Scott explains resistance and legitimacy, not which arrangements lasted. His evidence is that the fixed claim was resented; it is not that communities under variable claims outlived others.

## The objection to pre-empt

- **Popkin.** *The Rational Peasant* (1979) answers Scott with individual calculation [not in library; acquire].
- **Fafchamps.** Fafchamps 1992 (*Economic Development and Cultural Change*, DOI 10.1086/452001) reconciles the two — rational peasants with a moral economy — and is the safer citation for the mechanism.
- **Henrich and McElreath.** "Are Peasants Risk-Averse Decision Makers?" (*Current Anthropology* 43, 2002) finds peasants risk-prone in some settings. That cuts against reading safety-first as risk aversion. The project rejects that reading too, so the finding helps it.
- **Kunreuther and Wright.** "Safety-First, Gambling, and the Subsistence Farmer" is a University of Pennsylvania working paper (Institute of Public Policy Analysis / Government Study Center), dated July 1974 and received by the library in May 1975.
  - It models safety-first as a lexicographic rule and says its definition "differs from the one used by Roy (1952)".
  - Its result is the one to keep. A farmer below a minimum acreage cannot meet his required return at an acceptable risk and "will be forced to gamble" on the high-variance crop. They call this the obverse of Friedman–Savage: gambling "in order to avoid poverty".
  - Near the barrier, then, safety-first predicts risk-taking, not caution. That squares Henrich and McElreath with Scott, and it is what a barrier model predicts and a globally concave utility does not.
