# Which peril gets a clause is set by the tradition's doctrinal problem

Split the loss into peril and market — the ship against the price — and ask which of the two each tradition writes down. The three partnership traditions answer differently, and the difference tracks what each of them was anxious about rather than how the contracts actually worked.

**Latin: the peril is written, the market is inferred.** The Ragusan and Venetian notarial clause is *dicti solidi sunt ad periculum dicti [N.] maris et gentis clarefactum* — it names the peril (sea, piracy, seizure), names the bearer, and says nothing at all about price movement (Held 2025, 20). That market loss also falls on the commendator has to be recovered from the *absence* of any repayment obligation.

**Islamic: the same, by a different route.** Al-Sarakhsī's rule is undifferentiated — "losses incurred in the venture are the responsibility of the investor" (Ramli 2018, 95) — but the sunnaic warrant behind it, the ḥadīth of al-ʿAbbās, itemises perils: no sea, no oasis, no live animals. Peril named, market silent.

**Rabbinic: it flips.** Maimonides makes the *ʿisqa* manager answerable for "half of all **trading** losses to the partnership capital" (Ackerman-Lieberman 2011, 663). The market side is the explicit one; the peril side is left to bailment law, where the manager's status as a *paid* bailee has to be reasoned through before anything can be said about a shipwreck.

## The explanation is doctrinal pressure, not commercial practice

*Ribbit* forces the Talmudic discussion onto the return on capital: the whole *pelga milveh u-pelga pikkadon* engineering exists to stop the investor drawing a return on money without exposure ([[isqa]], [[Ribbit mandates exposure rather than forbidding gain]]). What has to be pinned down is therefore the **trading** outcome. A *periculum* clause and a voyage ḥadīth, by contrast, are drafted against the sea. Each tradition writes down the variable its own prohibition made contentious and leaves the other to default rules.

## Why this matters beyond the point itself

Because it is a warning about a field. `articulation` in `loss_mitigation_forms` records whether a coded function was stated by the tradition in its own idiom. Across these four forms the field comes out asymmetric — `RB1` articulated and `RB2` inferred in the Latin and Islamic rows, the reverse in the Jewish one — and none of that asymmetry is about the institutions. **`articulation` is measuring the doctrinal pressure on the tradition, not the explicitness of the tradition.** Counts of `articulated` cells across traditions therefore compare theologies, not instruments.

A second, weaker corollary: peril and market are separately articulated only where they **diverge**. Harris states both halves in one sentence for the sea loan, because there the peril goes to the lender and the market stays with the borrower and the contract has to say so. Where the two coincide — the whole partnership family — the tradition draws one clause and lets the rest follow. Explicitness follows contrast, not importance.
