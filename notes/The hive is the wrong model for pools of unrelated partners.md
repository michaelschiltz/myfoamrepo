# The hive is the wrong model for pools of unrelated partners

**Boomsma and Gawne 2018 argue that every superorganism — ants, corbiculate bees, vespine wasps, higher termites — arose by one route: top-down, from a family founded by lifetime monogamous commitment, never by unrelated individuals "coming together." In their words, "bottom-up coming together completely lacks the prospect of ever realizing a major fraternal evolutionary transition." If that holds, the honey bee is the model of the household, not of the partnership. Pools of unrelated partners — the *qirāḍ*, the *commenda*, the *kō*, the joint-stock company — need a different biological analogue, and the transition literature has one: organismality reached by unrelated partners whose interests are aligned by something other than kinship. That is where the time-average argument can do work kinship cannot.**

## The route and its point of no return

Boomsma and Gawne (16–19, read in full) set three steps: founders sequestered as specialised breeders; some offspring committed to somatic roles with reduced fecundity; and the completion of a lifelong mated/unmated caste split, the "point of no return" after which no worker is totipotent. Lifetime monogamy is the necessary condition because it makes relatedness to offspring and to siblings exactly equal, so that Hamilton's rule reduces to b/c > 1. Polyandry, policing and queen adoption came *after* the transition, as secondary elaborations. Aggregations of non-kin and chimeric cell groups have produced only simple and temporary collectives.

## Two institutional readings

- **The *ie* fits the route.** A household founded on a lifetime commitment, perpetuated by succession and adoption, with members whose roles are fixed for life inside it, is the top-down case ([[The ie as entity-shielding]], [[The fictive household supplied partition and perpetuity without legal personality]]). Adoption into the *ie* resembles queen adoption — a secondary elaboration that the unit survives because it was constituted first.
- **The irreversibility is capital lock-in.** The point of no return is the members' irreversible loss of the capacity to reproduce on their own account. The institutional analogue is the member's loss of the right to withdraw a share: capital lock-in ([[Arthur's lock-in and capital lock-in are different objects]] keeps the legal sense apart from the dynamic one). Lock-in is the institutional point of no return. This is an analogy of structure; it predicts that forms with full lock-in should be the ones that do not revert to dissolvable partnerships.

## The right analogue for partnerships

Queller and Strassmann 2009 (read in full) define organismality by near-unanimous cooperation and near-absent conflict, and list among the traits *not* essential to it "clonality or high relatedness" and "membership in the same species"; mutualisms such as figs and their pollinators may qualify. Boomsma and Gawne call this continuum approach "unsatisfactory", because it lumps domains that a transition separates. The disagreement is live. For the project it settles one thing: if unrelated partners ever form a unit, the alignment must come from somewhere other than kinship. Under multiplicative dynamics, the coincidence of member and pool growth rates on the long horizon is such a source ([[Pooling raises the rate and individuation draws the boundary]]). That is a claim about mutualism-type transitions, and it should be made about them, not borrowed from the hive.

## Limits

Boomsma and Gawne write about fraternal transitions only, and their necessity claim rests on comparative evidence that the transition happened only where lifetime monogamy was ancestral. Gardner and Grafen 2009 reach a parallel formal conclusion — group adaptation is fully justified only for clonal groups and partly for groups with complete repression of competition. Neither speaks to institutions, and the transfer is structural, not evidential.
