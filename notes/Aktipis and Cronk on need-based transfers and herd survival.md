# Aktipis and Cronk on need-based transfers and herd survival

The Human Generosity Project cluster:

- Aktipis, Cronk and de Aguiar, "Risk-Pooling and Herd Survival: An Agent-Based Model of a Maasai Gift-Giving System," *Human Ecology* 39 (2011), 131–140. DOI 10.1007/s10745-010-9364-9.
- **Aktipis, de Aguiar, Flaherty, Iyer, Sonkoi and Cronk, "Cooperation in an Uncertain World: For the Maasai of East Africa, Need-Based Transfers Outperform Account-Keeping in Volatile Environments," *Human Ecology* 44:3 (2016), 353–364. DOI 10.1007/s10745-016-9823-z. Read in full.**
- Campennì, Cronk and Aktipis, "Correlated Disasters and Need-Based Transfers: The Limits of Risk Pooling Systems in Simulated Ecologies," bioRxiv (2017). DOI 10.1101/230607.
- Cronk and Aktipis, "Design Principles for Risk-Pooling Systems," *Nature Human Behaviour* 5 (2021), 825–833. DOI 10.1038/s41562-021-01121-9.

**The template design for the project: an ethical rule evaluated by survival, with an explicit barrier.**

## What the 2016 paper does

*Osotua* ("umbilical cord") is the Maasai gift relationship. "Ask only if you are in need and only for what is needed, and give if you are able to do so without threatening your own survival" (p. 353). Transfers create no debt. The model compares this rule with *esile* account-keeping, which tracks debts with a credit limit and repayment.

- **The barrier is explicit.** A herd below 64 head for two consecutive years is removed (pp. 355, 361–362).
- **The outcome is survival.** The model reports the proportion of herds surviving up to 100 years, median herd duration, and 50-year survival as the payoff.
- **Result.** Need-based pairs outlast account-keeping pairs and pairs with no transfers. They pool risk more (within-pair survival correlation 0.54 against 0.40) and end up more equal (Gini 0.418 against 0.516). In the 50-year payoff matrix, need-based transfer dominates account-keeping. The advantage is clearest at intermediate volatility, and requires generosity of at least 80 percent (p. 357).

## Why it matters

- **The ethical rule is conditioned on the giver's barrier.** "Give if you are able … without threatening your own survival" is a norm that writes the absorbing state into its own text. Nothing in the religious-law literature is this explicit, and it is the cleanest proof that a moral rule can be read as a survival constraint without anachronism.
- **Debt-keeping loses to need.** The rule that keeps no accounts beats the one that does, in volatile environments. That parallels the doctrinal hostility to the priced-certain return: a credit relation that insists on repayment regardless of state is the one that kills.
- **The limits are the vault's ρ.** The authors confine the result to asynchronous, unpredictable shocks: "When individuals' needs all occur at the same time, neither account-keeping nor need-based transfers beyond close kin would likely be adaptive" (p. 359). The 2017 paper models the correlated case. This is [[Pooling's sign is set by the correlation of its baskets]], independently arrived at.
- **Verification matters.** The system works because cattle are public and hard to hide; where resources can be concealed it is open to cheating (p. 360). That is the verification problem of [[Gharar excludes designed-in unverifiability]] in pastoral form.

## Caution

This is an agent-based model calibrated on ethnography, not historical evidence of survival. It establishes that the design *can* be read this way and what a test would look like. It does not establish that *osotua* persisted because of the survival it confers.
