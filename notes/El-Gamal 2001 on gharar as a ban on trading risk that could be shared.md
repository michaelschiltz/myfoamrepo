# El-Gamal 2001 on gharar as a ban on trading risk that could be shared

Mahmoud A. El-Gamal, "An Economic Explication of the Prohibition of Gharar in Classical Islamic Jurisprudence." The PDF held is the working-paper version: "First version: May 2, 2001", 28 pp., prepared for the 4th International Conference on Islamic Economics, Leicester, August 2000. Published in *Islamic Economic Studies* 8:2 (2001), 29–58 [volume and pages of the published version unverified]. Read in full. Companion: "An Economic Explication of the Prohibition of Ribā in Classical Islamic Jurisprudence," working paper, 2 May 2001, 20 pp.

**The nearest neighbour to the project's reading of *gharar*. The argument is behavioural — it runs on prospect theory — not expected utility, as this note first said.**

## The argument, verified

- **Gharar is "trading in risk" (abstract, p. 1).** "Trading in risk" is "generally inefficient relative to other forms of risk sharing", so where a contract can reach its aim either way, the prohibition should apply. Where risk-trading is integral and needed (*salam*, *istiṣnāʿ*), look for a risk-sharing substitute (for example "financial vs. mutual insurance").
- **The criterion is not zero-sumness (pp. 2, 6–7).** El-Gamal rejects reading *gharar* as "the degree of zero-sumness of an exchange": some zero-sum exchanges are permitted, and some contracts are forbidden without any zero-sum component ("two sales in one", *bayʿatayn fī bayʿa*). "It is not zero-sumness which is being forbidden."
- **The mechanism is bounded rationality (pp. 7, 15–19).**
  - Prospect-theoretic agents — with reference points, loss aversion and probability weighting — accept risk-trading offers that are inefficient relative to the available risk-sharing ones. The worked example is on pp. 18–19.
  - The prohibition is therefore efficiency-enhancing paternalism: it stops boundedly rational parties from contracting into the dominated form (p. 17).
  - The empirical anchors are managers' risk-seeking below target (Fiegenbaum) and Camerer's survey of anomalies.

**The *ribā* companion runs the same way.** It works from Ibn Rushd's *maqāṣid* reasoning in *Bidāyat al-Mujtahid*. The tricks for avoiding *ribā al-faḍl* are "pre-commitment mechanisms" that mark exchanges to market. *Ribā al-nasīʾa* is explained by "individual discounting anomalies" and dynamically inconsistent behaviour (abstract, p. 1).

## How it sits beside the vault

- **Beside [[Gharar excludes designed-in unverifiability]]** El-Gamal adds a third function of *gharar*: verification, risk allocation, and protection from one's own biases. The three readings agree on which contracts fall, and that makes the refusal of the sea loan over-determined.
- **The method is the one the vault refuses.** It explains a doctrine by adding psychology to the expectation operator, which is [[Answering EU's failures with more psychology instantiates the complaint]] in a fiqh setting. The time-average version needs no biased agents: a fair transfer of risk lowers both parties' growth rates ([[A fair wager lowers both trajectories which maysir forbids and skin in the game permits]]).
- **El-Gamal's rejection of zero-sumness bears on that note.** It is *gharar* he says is not a zero-sum test; *maysir* he keeps as the extreme case of selling risk (p. 6). The fair-wager note's claim concerns *maysir* and survives, but it should cite El-Gamal's distinction rather than talk loosely of "zero-sum" doctrine.
- **A lead.** The *ribā* paper's appeal to discounting anomalies meets the ergodicity-economics derivation of apparent hyperbolic discounting from multiplicative dynamics [verify: Adamou, Berman, Mavroyiannis and Peters on the microfoundations of discounting]. If that holds, El-Gamal's "anomaly" is a time-average effect, and the *ribā al-nasīʾa* argument can be restated without bias.
