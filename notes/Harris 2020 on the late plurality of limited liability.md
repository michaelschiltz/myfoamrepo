# Harris 2020 on the late plurality of limited liability

Ron Harris, "A new understanding of the history of limited liability: an invitation for theoretical reframing," *Journal of Institutional Economics* 16(5), 2020, 643–664. DOI 10.1017/S1744137420000181.

Harris dismantles the linear narrative of limited liability — "widely considered a cornerstone of the business corporation." He denies that limited liability was born with the first joint-stock corporations around 1600, and denies that it became dominant with the mid-nineteenth-century limited-liability acts. His claim: limited liability became a *separate* corporate attribute, distinct from legal personality, only around 1800, and a *uniform* attribute of all corporations only in the twentieth century. Corporations, stock markets, and a corporate economy flourished for centuries before modern limited liability was established, so the economic theory that treats limited liability as the corporation's defining efficiency needs revisiting — and he points explicitly to "viable future liability regimes."

Why it supports [[The corporation is not the omega - the naties are an undisplaced niche]]: the argument is *internal to Harris* and cuts against his own convergence-toward-the-joint-stock-company frame. The attribute usually named as the corporation's telos is shown to be late, plural, and contingent — assembled piecemeal, not present at a founding and not uniform until the twentieth century. If the corporation's own cornerstone is a late and non-linear accretion, the corporation cannot be the endpoint of a scalar ascent; it is one configuration in an open design space whose liability regimes remain, on Harris's own telling, still to be settled. Harris the historian of limited liability refutes the teleology that Harris the narrator of convergence leans upon.
