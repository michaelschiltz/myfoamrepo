# Under multiplicative dynamics the restoring force is an institutional achievement

In reallocating geometric Brownian motion — individual wealth grows multiplicatively, and a share is pooled and returned in equal parts at rate $\tau$ — rescaled wealth has a stationary, ergodic distribution only if $\tau > 0$. Under pure geometric Brownian motion ($\tau = 0$) relative wealth is non-ergodic, and for $\tau < 0$ wealth is repelled from the mean. Berman, Peters and Adamou (2021) fit $\tau$ to United States data for 1917–2012 and find it positive until the 1980s and negative, and growing in magnitude, since; even where positive, the implied equilibration times run to decades or centuries. Stojkoski shows that in the mixing regime the relaxation time scales as $1/\tau$, so that mobility and mixing are one quantity seen twice. Bouchaud and Farmer (2023) bring quasi-non-ergodic wealth dynamics into the *Journal of Political Economy*.

Read through this model, the stationarity that Clark and the mobility literature presume is not a default property of a society. It is the output of reallocation — of pooling — and its strength is set by the institutions that pool. The mainstream treats mean reversion as natural and persistence as what needs explaining. Under multiplicative dynamics the order is reversed: divergence is the default, and a restoring force is an institutional achievement with a history of its own. That is Part 1's thesis — pooling as an operation on the dynamics — seen from the side of its absence. See [[Pooling approaches the infinite ensemble and perpetual succession the infinite horizon]] and [[The expectation value describes a pool that re-shares not a population]].

Clark and Cummins (2026) are compatible with this. Wealth shocks that vanish within three generations in England before 1880 amount to an estimate that the effective $\tau$ was positive in that regime. The disagreement is over whether the parameter is a law or a property of a regime.

## Consequence for Clark's place in the project

Decision log D2 treats Clark as a stress test for the method. He is also the substantive opponent of Objective 1's mechanism. A persistence rate that genuinely does not respond to institutions is the strongest available evidence against the claim that pooling institutions set the restoring force; one that moves with regimes is evidence for it. The question is the homogeneity of the law across regimes ([[Time-homogeneity not stationarity is what removes time]]), and Clark's estimates across countries and centuries are the data. Taken with [[A persistence parameter stable across regime change indicts the measure]] the dilemma is sharp: either the invariance is real and tells against the mechanism, or it is an artefact of the measure. The project cannot leave both open.
