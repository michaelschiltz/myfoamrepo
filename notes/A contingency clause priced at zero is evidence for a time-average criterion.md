# A contingency clause priced at zero is evidence for a time-average criterion

Of 393 *asientos* for which every clause and repayment scenario can be read, 270 carry at least one contingency, giving 408 scenarios in all: fleet arrival, the yield of named tax streams, a king's option to postpone, a banker's option to take repayment in *juros*, and collateral execution (2014: 214–15). Drelichman and Voth then report the price. "In aggregate, contingencies do not affect the returns substantially. The median change in the cost of a contract is close to zero" (221). Contracts carrying a tax-shortfall clause were 1.6 percentage points *cheaper* in the baseline than the average contract, and the authors state the conclusion plainly: "bankers were willing to offer this type of 'insurance' without a premium" (224).

Under expected utility this is an anomaly requiring a preference parameter to absorb it. The Genoese would have to be systematically more averse to variance than a monarch with an empire behind him, and would have to supply that aversion for nothing. Drelichman and Voth do not attempt the manoeuvre. They name the arrangement risk sharing, which is correct, and leave the price reported rather than explained.

Under a time-average criterion the zero price is the prediction rather than the anomaly. A clause that leaves the expectation of the cash flows unchanged while reducing their dispersion raises the growth rate of the capital exposed to them, and raises it for both counterparties, because it alters the dynamics both are subject to instead of moving a loss from one balance sheet to the other. A contract that raises both parties' growth rates can be written at zero premium, or below zero, and still be worth writing by both. The insurance puzzle, which asks how a contract with negative expectation for the buyer can be worth buying, does not arise (Peters and Adamou 2022; see [[Pricing risk does not remove it]]).

The discriminating evidence is the asymmetry in the authors' own Table 23. Clauses triggered by verifiable events outside either party's control are nearly free: invoking the fleet contingency changes the return by 0.4 per cent, invoking the tax-shortfall clause reduces it by 1.7, and contracts carrying the latter start 1.6 points below average. The king's-discretion clause, which permits nonpayment with no externally verifiable trigger, costs him 8.4 per cent all told once baseline and contingency cost are combined (224, 226).

Moral hazard explains why the discretionary clause was dear, and that explanation stands. It does not explain why the verifiable clause was free. The free clause is the observation that needs a criterion, and only one of the two criteria supplies it.

## Why this is the sharpest case in the corpus

The two theories are not usually separable on historical evidence, because both predict that risk-sharing forms exist. Here they separate on price, and the price is in the archive.
