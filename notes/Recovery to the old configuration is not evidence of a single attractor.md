# Recovery to the old configuration is not evidence of a single attractor

Davis and Weinstein (2002) read the return of Japanese cities to their pre-war ranks after the Allied bombing as relaxation to an attractor fixed by locational fundamentals, and their 2008 paper turns the reading into a formal test that finds no multiple equilibria across 114 cities and eight manufacturing industries. The inference from return to uniqueness does not follow.

Takeda and Yamagishi (2026) find that the centre of Hiroshima recovered within about five years of its destruction, that the recovery is not explained by the centre's fundamental locational characteristics, and that a calibrated dynamic urban model attributes it to strong agglomeration together with expectations of recovery. A return produced by coordinated expectations is the selection of one equilibrium among several: the old configuration is restored because it is expected to be, not because it is the only stable state. Reversion establishes a restoring force; it does not establish that the force points to a unique place.

Two results from the same literature show that the restoring force is regime-dependent, which bears directly on WP3's condition of regime homogeneity. Brakman, Garretsen and Schramm (2004) find mean reversion in West German but not in East German city growth after the bombing, and attribute the difference to the post-war economic systems. Mikhailova (2012) finds the effect of the Second World War on Soviet city growth gone within twenty-five years, while the effect of Gulag camps is permanent — both kinds of case in a single design.

The converse is recorded at [[Distinguish strong from weak ergodicity breaking]]: Stachurski shows that persistence can coexist with formal ergodicity; here reversion coexists with multiple equilibria. Neither observation settles the regime on its own.
