# Ford and Kay 2023 on growth-optimal decision making

Source note for the most serious published critique of ergodicity economics, and for the three-part exchange around it. All three documents read in full; all are open access.

**The exchange.**

- **Ford, Matthew C., and John A. Kay. 2023.** "The Limitations of Growth-Optimal Approaches to Decision Making Under Uncertainty." *Econ Journal Watch* 20 (2): 314–334.
- **Hulme, Oliver, Arne Vanhoyweghen, Colm Connaughton, Ole Peters, Simon Steinkamp, Alexander Adamou, Dominik Baumann, Vincent Ginis, Bert Verbruggen, James Price, and Benjamin Skjold. 2023.** "Reply to 'The Limitations of Growth-Optimal Approaches to Decision Making Under Uncertainty.'" *Econ Journal Watch* 20 (2): 335–348.
- **Ford and Kay. 2024.** "Rejoinder on Ergodicity Economics." *Econ Journal Watch* 21 (1): 69–78.

**Why this outranks the other two critiques.** Kay was the first dean of the Saïd Business School, a Fellow of the British Academy, and co-author with Mervyn King of *Radical Uncertainty*; Ford worked on that book. The critique is published rather than a preprint, it drew an eleven-author reply from the ergodicity-economics group itself, and Ford and Kay **concede an error of their own** in the rejoinder. Compare [[Toda 2023 on ergodicity economics as pseudoscience]], which is an unrefereed polemic whose headline its own §IV refutes. Ford and Kay are not polemicists and the vault should not treat them as one.

## What Ford and Kay argue

**The framing.** EE is traditional growth optimisation — Kelly 1956, Latané — with a new metaphysical justification. Two claims distinguish it: that it needs no reference to individual psychology, and that it makes better metaphysical sense than the alternatives. Both are attacked.

**The Samuelson extension.** Their technical core. Samuelson (1971) showed the limit is an inappropriate guide for finite processes. Ford and Kay extend it: growth-optimal behaviour is **incompatible with any utility function**, hence necessarily violates the EUT axioms. Any strictly concave region means declining an additive gamble with slightly positive time average; any strictly convex region means accepting one with slightly negative time average; and linear utility accepts the Peters bet, which EE rejects. No utility function survives all three.

**Completeness fails three ways.** EE cannot rank an additive gamble against a multiplicative one; a length-1 gamble admits both additive and multiplicative descriptions and receives different orderings though the object is identical; and their constructed time-varying gambles $G_+ \succ G_C \succ G_-$ have wealth orderings that reverse at every finite date, with **no** minimum length at which the ranking becomes sensible. The third is the sharpest and Hulme et al. do not answer it. Consequences at [[EE cannot rank across dynamics and the comparative project must not need it to]].

**The metaphysical rebuttal.** EUT is a representation of preferences, not a description of a process; the expectation operator invokes no multiverse; and EE *also* uses the expectation operator, after a transformation. This is [[Defending expected utility by axiomatics forfeits its normative claim]] arriving from a second direction, and better argued here than in the Doctor–Wakker–Wang supplement.

**No rule of application.** EE gives no guidance on when a gamble is long enough. Their example: a monthly coin toss for ten years, $+\$2/-\$1$ against $+\$102/-\$100$. EE prefers the second on additive time average; Ford and Kay predict nobody would take it, and note that the two variables which would change the prediction — the agent's wealth level and near risk-neutrality — are variables in EUT and not in EE.

**The experiments.** Appendix B dismantles both. Meder et al. (2021) applies EUT myopically and ignores subjects' external wealth. The internal point is the good one: since experimental money leaves the laboratory into a multiplicative world, **EE's own logic should predict $\rho = 1$ on the additive day rather than the $\rho = 0$ it is credited with predicting.** Vanhoyweghen et al. (2022) is worse — the safer lottery second-order stochastically dominates, so any risk-averse expected-utility maximiser makes the same choice as an EE agent and the design cannot discriminate at all.

## What the eleven-author reply establishes

Two things, and the first is a genuine correction.

**Ford and Kay's opening structural claim was false, and they withdraw it.** They had written that under EE "final wealth will almost always be what the time average predicts" — that the model operates where there is no measurable uncertainty. Hulme et al. show that the growth rate converges while **terminal wealth diverges**: in the Peters coin toss the variance of the averaged growth rate vanishes as $1/T$ while the variance of wealth diverges exponentially. Ford and Kay accept the correction in the rejoinder.

**The strongest pro-EE argument in the whole literature, and it belongs in our text.** Hulme et al. establish that the EE–EUT mapping holds only where the utility function is chosen to *be* the ergodicity transformation, $u = f$. Where $f \neq u$: agents maximising the time-average growth rate maximise utility as time passes, while agents maximising expected utility **do not maximise utility over time**. Their worked case is the Peters coin toss with linear utility — the EUT agent accepts, loses money with probability approaching one, and since utility is monotonically increasing in wealth, loses utility. *Expected* utility rises while actual utility falls. That EUT fails to maximise the very object it defines as the goal is a consequence of non-ergodicity, and it is the sharpest single statement of the framework's claim.

On completeness they answer that the dynamic is a hidden variable: once specified, EE preferences are complete, exactly as EUT preferences are complete once a utility function is specified.

## What survives the rejoinder

Ford and Kay concede the terminal-wealth error and maintain three things, of which two are strong.

- **The "guarantee" is a limit statement.** $P(w_t < w_0 \text{ for all sufficiently large } t) = 1$, but for any finite $t$ the probability is below one. Latané's "probability of adverse dominance" is the honest framing. Bears directly on [[The growth-rate criterion is asymptotic and the horizon comparison cuts both ways]].
- **Why rank by the most probable outcome?** People fasten seatbelts, insure houses, buy lottery tickets, apply for jobs they will probably not get, and invest in startups that will probably fail. There is no principled answer to "how probable must an outcome be before we disregard all others?" short of "certain."
- The hidden-variable defence is rejected on the ground that the hidden variable "has no impact on what actually occurs" — which is the point that [[Ford and Kay concede the condition under which their objection lapses]] turns around.
