# Mandeville's hive is the game-theoretic hive

**The hive has served both sides of the argument the proposal makes. The classical hive, from Virgil and Seneca to Marcus Aurelius, gives the whole priority over the part. Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees* (1714) inverts it: public prosperity issues from private vice, and the hive that is made honest collapses. That second hive — order from self-interested parts acting under fixed rules, no whole presupposed — is structurally the hive of game theory, and Hayek claimed it as an ancestor of spontaneous order. An epigraph from 6.54 takes the first side. The ergodicity result gives that side an argument neither tradition had: the bee's interest and the swarm's coincide on the long horizon, without the Stoic subordination of the part or Mandeville's alchemy of vice.**

## Consequences

The epigraph is not neutral, and one sentence in Part 1 acknowledging the rival hive would forestall a reader who knows Mandeville [decide after reading Dale 2026]. And Rodgers's warning against naturalising analogies is the historian's version of Cremer and Sixt's insistence on phenomenological analogy ([[Social immunity is a consequence of individuation not evidence for it]]): the hive is to be used as the image of a formal structure, and said to be one. [[Stoic loci on cooperation - only two argue from interdependence]] already separates membership loci from interdependence loci; Mandeville belongs to neither, which is why he is the true opponent.
