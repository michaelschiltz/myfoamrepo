# Rubin 2009 on the interest ban as a curb on overborrowing under Church insurance

Jared Rubin, "Social Insurance, Commitment, and the Origin of Law: Interest Bans in Early Christianity," *Journal of Law and Economics* 52:4 (2009), 761–786. DOI 10.1086/595796. Read in full.

> **CORRECTED 2026-10-04, same day.** The first version of this note, written from the abstract, said the ban was a commitment device *supporting* in-group insurance. That is wrong, and so was the note's original title, "Rubin 2009 on interest bans as in-group insurance in early Christianity" (renamed the same day). Rubin's ban is a curb on the **moral hazard that the Church's insurance created**. It argues *against* the insurance reading of Glaeser and Scheinkman, and its model has an explicit absorbing state.

**The historical puzzle (p. 762).** There was no Christian prohibition of interest before the fourth century. The Church banned interest only *after* it became wealthy enough to provide social insurance. That timing "undermines hypotheses explaining interest laws as a primitive means of providing social insurance (see especially Posner 1980; Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998)."

**The model (pp. 765–766).**

- An infinitely lived insurer (the Church) faces overlapping generations of agents endowed with income below subsistence.
- Agents reach subsistence either by borrowing or by a Church transfer. "If agents do not reach subsistence, they drop out of the economy (that is, there is perfect enforcement of loans, and on default agents are enslaved, are sent to prison, starve, or are relegated to debt peonage, and so forth)." **That is an absorbing barrier, written into the model.**
- The Church "derives utility from providing social insurance—maximizing the number of agents in the economy." **The insurer's objective is a survival count.**

**The mechanism (p. 766).**

- A wealthy Church cannot credibly withhold transfers from those who overborrow. Agents therefore borrow beyond subsistence, "since they know that the Church will provide them subsistence in the future."
- A poor Church can ration its transfers and so punish overborrowing. A rich one cannot, and turns to the ban. "The ban is effective because it limits (over-)borrowing, not because it helps smooth consumption (as in Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998)."
- **History (pp. 770–772).** The fourth-century wealth shock, Nicaea (325) and the Cappadocian Fathers on lending to those "on the edge of subsistence".
- **Conclusion (p. 774).** Insurance "encourages overborrowing because agents do not suffer the full consequences of default."

## Why it matters — more than the first version allowed

- **It is the project's skin-in-the-game argument, in a model, about a doctrine.** The Church's insurance lifts the absorbing barrier off the borrower's trajectory. The borrower then behaves as an agent shielded from his own barrier, and the ban restores exposure by another route. In the vault's terms ([[Skin in the game]]; [[Entity shielding and owner shielding protect different trajectories]]), the ban exists *because* the barrier was relocated onto the insurer.
- **Its rule is a substitute for the insurer's lost credibility, not a substitute for missing insurance.** That inverts Glaeser and Scheinkman's sign, and the inversion is testable by timing. For the project, that makes it a stronger anchor than Glaeser and Scheinkman.
- **The gap it leaves is the dynamic.** The barrier is a fixed subsistence threshold in a two-period agent problem. Nothing compounds, and survival enters as a count the insurer maximises, not as a time-average property of a trajectory. See [[Doctrine has been read as subsistence protection but never through the time average]].

**The persistence companion.** Rubin's later papers explain why the ban *persisted* in Islam and eroded in Christian Europe by the rulers' dependence on religious legitimacy. They are "Institutions, the Rise of Commerce and the Persistence of Laws" (*Economic Journal* 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2011.02456.x), "The Lender's Curse" (*Journal of Economic History* 2008) and "Bills of Exchange" (*Explorations in Economic History* 2010). Origin and filter are separate questions, answered by the same author: [[Taleb reads survival as evidence about the survivor and we read it as evidence about the filter]].
