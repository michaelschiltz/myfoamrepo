# Temin and Voth 2008 test predictions Glaeser and Scheinkman did not make

Peter Temin and Hans-Joachim Voth, "Interest Rate Restrictions in a Natural Experiment: Loan Allocation and the Change in the Usury Laws in 1714," *Economic Journal* 118 (2008), 743–758. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2008.02140.x. Read in full (October 2026).

**The standard empirical citation against the insurance reading of usury laws. It establishes that the 1714 ceiling bound. It does not refute Glaeser and Scheinkman, because its "G&S predictions" are its own construction and its main findings are ones G&S anticipated. Its ensemble reasoning is real but secondary: it infers borrower welfare from a lender's loan book.**

## What the paper does

- **The event.** 13 Anne c. 15 lowered the legal maximum from 6% to 5% from 29 September 1714 (p. 745, n. 3).
- **The data.** 877 loans to 542 clients in Hoare's Bank ledgers, roughly 1690–1725. The clients were "noblemen, officers, church officials and wealthy traders"; they "clearly were not representative of the English population" (p. 746).
- **The findings (Tables 2–6).**
  - The median rate fell from 6% to 5%, and 92% of loans were made at the ceiling (p. 747).
  - Average loan size rose from £640 to £1,259 (p. 750). Being "known" (in the DNB or Cokayne) was worth an extra £1,025 after 1714 (Table 3).
  - Retained customers were more aristocratic and less often female (Table 5).
  - Collateralised lending rose; duration fell from 940 to 680 days.
  - Identified defaults went from 13 before 1714 to none after (p. 754).
- **The conclusion.** "The Glaeser and Scheinkman rationale for usury laws receives no support from our data" (pp. 755–756). The authors read the episode as financial repression.

## What it establishes

**The law bound.** The rate fell by exactly one point, there is no trace of offsetting deposits or fees, and the profit accounts cannot be squared with evasion (pp. 746–747, n. 8). For 1714 London, scepticism about the enforcement of usury laws is misplaced. That holds under any framework.

## Where it fails as a test of Glaeser and Scheinkman

- **The predictions are not G&S's.** Temin and Voth derive that, under G&S, "access to credit should have widened" and "the minimum loan size should drop" (p. 748). G&S say the opposite in two places.
  - **Rationing is built into the model (G&S p. 6).** The ceiling sits below the market rate, so "the actual quantity of loans is determined by the willingness of the rich to lend." Less lending is the cost of the insurance, not a sign that it failed.
  - **Excluding high-risk loans is a stated second function (G&S p. 12, nn. 24–25).** "Interest-rate restrictions may play a second function of stopping the occurrence of high-risk loans," where default has social costs or "bankrupt individuals become wards of the state."
  - So Temin and Voth's central result — the bank shed marginal borrowers and defaults went to zero — is G&S's second function at work.
- **The one damaging finding is a case G&S flag.** The concentration of credit on the "known" borrowers is the costly case of G&S n. 16: if "a particular class of poorer agents received all of the loans," usury laws become "much more costly." It tells you which parameter case 1714 London fell into. It does not reject the mechanism.
- **They quote Smith against themselves.** Smith (p. 745) defends a cap near the market rate because it shuts out "prodigals and projectors" and keeps "sober people" borrowing. That is the cap as a screen, and the paper then counts the screening as a cost.
- **Wrong population.** G&S's theory concerns consumption loans to the temporarily poor, and their evidence is 1950 US consumer-finance law. A West End goldsmith's ledger that never contained the poor cannot falsify insurance for the poor.

## The ensemble reasoning

- **The unit is the lender's loan book.** Borrowers dropped after 1714 vanish from the data. Whether they borrowed elsewhere, defaulted elsewhere, or did fine without credit is unobserved. A conclusion about borrower welfare is drawn from the lender's cross-section.
- **"Access to credit" counts as a good in itself.** This is the deepest ensemble premise in the paper: more fixed-claim credit is better for the marginal borrower. Under the barrier reading, keeping a fragile household out of fixed-claim debt can be the protective function. That is the line through [[Hudson 2002 on clean slates as protection of the free population]], [[Rubin 2009 on interest bans as in-group insurance in early Christianity]] and G&S p. 12. The paper cannot consider it, because it never follows a borrower's path.
- **The subsidy is valued per loan in expectation:** £4.3 at the median, £21.9 at the mean (p. 752).
- **The debate is ensemble against ensemble.** G&S defend the cap with ex-ante expected utility behind a veil (their eq. 1). Temin and Voth attack it with loan-book averages. Neither looks at a trajectory. The project's contribution is not to take a side but to change the outcome variable ([[The split is over whether the explanandum is a state or a trajectory]]).

## Survival logic for the bank, ensemble logic for the borrowers

- **The bank's own ruin drives the mechanism.** A lower ceiling lowers "the maximum default rate it can tolerate" (p. 748), and the bank retreats from risk.
- **The survivorship defence is a non sequitur (p. 746).** They argue that Hoare's being "not particularly profitable" reduces survivorship bias. But a bank that survived despite low profits is still a survivor, and more likely a cautious one.
- **The sample is selected on the response being measured.**
  - The authors concede: "We know little about the many banks operating in the 1710s that did not survive." Hoare's may have been one of the few whose "blue-blooded customer list" allowed it to adapt (p. 756).
  - The only archive complete enough to study (p. 743, n. 1) belongs to the bank whose strategy was the retreat to the connected. That is [[Persistence samples are conditioned on non-absorption]] in a single firm.

## Statistical fragility

- Adjusted R² runs from 0.02 to 0.06.
- The pre-1714 premium for being "known" is significant only at 10% (n. 9).
- There is no break test; the authors say the data frequency does not allow one (p. 749).
- There are 191 post-1714 loans against 686 before.
- Defaults are identified by a heuristic (sale of collateral or transfer to a partner): "fewer than 15" in all.
- **The collateral "jump" is partly an artefact of the base period** (Table 6).
  - By number of loans, the collateralised share after 1714 is 43%, close to 1700–04 (45%). The outlier is the 12% of 1710–14.
  - By value, the shares move with a few large mortgages, in a loan-size distribution with a Gini of 0.72.

## What a barrier test of 1714 would need

The population at risk, not a lender's book: absorptions among borrowers before and after 1714. Bankruptcy commissions are one candidate (Hoppit, *Risk and Failure in English Business 1700–1800*, Cambridge, 1987 [not in library; acquire]). They are confounded by the peace of 1713 and the 1720 bubble, and they cover traders, not households near subsistence.
