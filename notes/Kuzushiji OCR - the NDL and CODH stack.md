# *Kuzushiji* OCR — the NDL and CODH stack

The Japanese pipeline. Entirely separate from [[Transkribus - managed HTR platform]], which is Latin-script and does nothing here. For *Clearing and Settling the Realm* this is the consequential tooling: *komonjo* 古文書 and *kokiroku* 古記録 in early-modern cursive are the material, and their illegibility at scale is the reason the early-modern Japanese archive is undertransacted rather than unavailable.

## NDL古典籍OCR (NDL Kotenseki OCR)

Developed by **NDL Lab** at the National Diet Library, open source, in development since 2022. Currently **ver. 3** (Feb 2024) — ver. 3's advance over ver. 2 was layout recognition on *kanseki* 漢籍 material.

Developer evaluation reports **average ~92% character accuracy** on the full model.

**NDL古典籍OCR-Lite** (Nov 2024) is the one to actually use. Near-identical functionality at roughly **2% accuracy cost**, and it is dramatically more usable:

- Runs **entirely locally, no cloud, no GPU required**.
- Desktop app for Windows, macOS and Linux; also a web app; also a CLI.
- Output as TXT (line-by-line), JSON and XML (**including layout coordinates and per-character recognition confidence**), and PDF with a transcript overlay.
- **Retrainable on your own data**, with published instructions for calibrating both layout and character recognition.
- Interface is Japanese-only. Not a constraint for you.

The confidence scores in the JSON/XML output are the underused feature: they let you route low-confidence lines to human review programmatically instead of proofreading everything. For a corpus-scale project that is the difference between feasible and not.

Distinct from **NDLOCR-Lite** (released 24 Feb 2026), which targets *modern* printed material and is also GPU-free. Different tool, confusingly similar name. NDL's own guidance is that for serious *kuzushiji* or *kanseki* work you use the *kotenseki* model, not the general one.

## Why it works on this material specifically

The model uses a **context-aware decoder** — it predicts each character from surrounding text rather than treating characters independently, resolving degraded or ambiguous glyphs by inference. This is unusually well suited to the **highly formulaic *sōrōbun* 候文 style**, where the epistolary and administrative register is heavily patterned. Financial and administrative documents are near the best case: the formulae are rigid.

**The failure mode is the direct cost of that strength, and it is the single most important line in this note.** Because errors are generated by a language model rather than by independent character classification, **the mistakes tend to be grammatically well-formed.** They do not look like errors. Single-character OCR produces obvious garbage that the eye catches; context-aware OCR produces plausible Japanese that says something other than what the document says.

For a book that will make quantitative claims about clearing and settlement from these documents, this is a live threat to the evidentiary base — an error class with high plausibility and low detectability, concentrated exactly where the formulae are most regular and therefore where the model is most confident. Numerals, sums, dates, place names and personal names are the highest-risk fields precisely because they are the least constrained by the surrounding formula. **Any figure that enters an argument should be verified against the image. Automate the routing with the confidence scores; do not automate the trust.**

## Reported accuracy

- Developer evaluation, full ver. 3: **~92% average**.
- Xia-Kang Ziyi (Kyushu), testing on domain–*bakufu* interaction manuscripts: **~97%**.

The gap is informative rather than contradictory — it is what you would expect when a general benchmark meets a formulaic administrative genre. Your material likely sits closer to the 97% case than the 92%, which strengthens the tooling case and does nothing to soften the warning above. At 97% character accuracy a 400-character page still carries ~12 errors.

## Alternatives

**CODH AI Kuzushiji OCR / Miwo みを** — ROIS-DS Center for Open Data in the Humanities. Free, web platform plus mobile app. Earliest and best known. Strong on *kuzushiji kana* and printed books; **accuracy drops significantly on kanji-heavy *komonjo***, which is most of what matters here. No bulk processing without special arrangement.

**Komonjo Camera 古文書カメラ** (Toppan) — has a dedicated *komonjo* mode and handles kanji-heavy manuscript material better than Miwo. But: **mobile app only, general availability limited to Japan, free tier capped at 30 pages/day**, no bulk processing. A lookup tool for a hard passage, not a pipeline.

**KotenOCR** (iOS, March 2026) — third-party app wrapping NDL古典籍OCR-Lite for phone capture. Same model, convenience packaging.

## Bottom line

**NDL古典籍OCR-Lite is the only one of these that is a pipeline rather than a lookup tool** — local execution, CLI, bulk processing, structured output with confidence scores, retrainable. The others are useful for checking a single difficult character. Reserve Miwo and Komonjo Camera for adjudicating passages the pipeline flags.

## To verify

- Whether ver. 3 remains current or a ver. 4 has appeared since Feb 2024.
- Practical performance on **account books and ledgers specifically** — tabular and columnar financial layouts are a layout-analysis problem, not a character-recognition problem, and every accuracy figure above is for running text. **This is the real unknown for this project and worth testing on a sample before committing.**
