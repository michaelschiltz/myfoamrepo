# Doctrine as survival technology: a reading path

*HistorEE briefing for project partners. Draft, 4 October 2026.*

## Why this briefing

Many religious and legal doctrines restrict credit and risk. Examples are the Christian usury ban, the Islamic prohibitions of *ribā* and *gharar*, the Jewish prohibition of *ribbit*, and the clean-slate proclamations of the ancient Near East. These rules look economically wasteful, and yet they lasted for centuries. A natural HistorEE hypothesis is that they were *survival technologies*: rules that kept households, and through them communities, away from an irreversible loss. In this reading, ethics is the gateway to survival.

A literature sweep in October 2026 asked how far this idea has already been pursued. It covered six deep searches, roughly 970 ranked papers, and 82 items shortlisted and read where it mattered. The answer is: further than we assumed, but in a specific and limited way. This briefing sets out the methodological debate the sweep uncovered. It then proposes an order of reading in which each text answers a question the previous one leaves open.

The short version:

- **The barrier is already in the literature.** At least two published models treat falling below subsistence as exit from the economy.
- **What is missing is the dynamic and the evidence.** Nobody has modelled compounding household wealth. So nobody has shown what a fixed claim does to a household's long-run growth rate when its income fluctuates multiplicatively. And nobody has measured whether communities that adopted these rules survived longer.
- **That is where HistorEE can contribute**: by changing the outcome variable, not by taking a side in the existing debate.

## The methodological debate in three positions

The literature evaluates the same doctrines in three different ways. Keeping them apart is the main thing a reader should take from this path.

**1. Expected utility across an ensemble: the insurance reading.** A ceiling on interest transfers income from lenders in good states to borrowers in bad ones. Behind a veil, with concave utility, this raises expected welfare. This is Glaeser and Scheinkman's model. The criterion is an average over a population of agents who do not yet know whether they will be rich or poor. Risk aversion does all the work.

**2. Survival as a constraint: the additive barrier reading.** Agents who fall below subsistence starve, are enslaved or drop out. A rule is good if it keeps more people above the line. Reed and Bekar's agents are risk-neutral, so the whole value of pooling comes from the barrier and none from concavity. Rubin's Church maximises the number of agents alive. This is a real advance on position 1, and in Reed and Bekar it comes close to the time-average view. Survival compounds over periods, the agent values its whole remaining life, and the Church ranks survival above everything else. But income is a fresh additive draw each period, there is no wealth that grows or shrinks, and the agent still maximises an expected discounted value. Above all, debt is harmless in the model: credit never pushes a borrower towards the barrier.

**3. The trajectory under multiplicative dynamics: the HistorEE reading.** A household's wealth compounds and fluctuates. What matters is the growth rate of a single household's path over time, not the average across households at a moment. Under these dynamics a fixed claim against volatile income lowers the time-average growth rate. It also raises the probability of absorption, and it does both even when the mean is unchanged. A claim that moves with the outcome does not. This is the ergodicity-economics point, and it is absent from the doctrinal literature. Peters and Adamou carry the mechanism as far as "moral sentiment" and stop there.

Each position predicts a different quantity, which matters for any empirical design:

| Reading | What a good rule produces | Where you would look |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance (ensemble) | Higher ex-ante expected welfare | Credit allocation, consumption smoothing |
| Additive barrier | Fewer exits in a bad year | Famine, default and enslavement counts in a crisis |
| Multiplicative trajectory | Fewer absorptions accumulated over time, concentrated among holders of fixed claims, with a gap that widens with volatility | Longitudinal household or community survival |

Two further debates cut across all three positions.

- **Origin is not persistence.** A rule may arise for one reason and survive for another. The persistence literature explains the survival of interest bans by rulers' need for religious legitimacy, by clerical control, or by rents to incumbents, not by survival value.
- **Function read back into origin.** A norm that has a function today need not have arisen because of it. The large-sample tests of the "Big Gods" hypothesis are the standing warning here.

## The reading path

Each step names what to read, what to look for, and the question it leaves for the next step.

### Step 0. The mechanism

Peters and Adamou 2022, "The ergodicity solution of the cooperation puzzle." Skip this if you already know it.

- **What to look for:** cooperation through pooling raises the time-average growth rate of every participant under multiplicative dynamics, without any appeal to altruism. The paper ends by saying that our moral sentiment "is in harmony with a careful formal analysis" (p. 12). It mentions land scattering, insurance, pensions and taxation, and no legal doctrine.
- **Question it leaves:** if moral sentiment tracks the time average, does *law* track it too? And how has the economics of law read these doctrines so far?

### Step 1. The standard account

Glaeser and Scheinkman 1998, "Neither a Borrower nor a Lender Be." Read pp. 1–6, 12 and 20–23.

- **What to look for:**
  - The model (pp. 5–6) is expected utility with strictly concave utility, no aggregate uncertainty, and a planner maximising welfare behind a veil (their equation 1). It is position 1 in its purest form.
  - Two passages go beyond the model. On p. 12 the ceiling has "a second function of stopping the occurrence of high-risk loans" when "bankrupt individuals become wards of the state."
  - On pp. 20–21 the biblical rules keep individuals from becoming "burdens on the community", and debt slavery removes the poor from their obligations (nn. 37–39).
  - The barrier is in the text but not in the model.
- **Question it leaves:** this is the paper everyone cites as the insurance reading. What happens when it is tested?

### Step 2. The standard refutation, and why it fails

Temin and Voth 2008, "Interest Rate Restrictions in a Natural Experiment." England lowered the legal interest ceiling from 6% to 5% in 1714. The authors study loans at Hoare's Bank before and after.

- **What to look for:**
  - The paper shows that the ceiling bound: 92% of loans were made at the limit (p. 747).
  - It then tests "predictions" of Glaeser and Scheinkman that the latter never made, such as wider access to credit and smaller minimum loans (p. 748). Glaeser and Scheinkman build rationing into their model (p. 6).
  - The paper's main findings — the bank shed marginal borrowers and defaults went to zero — are the "second function" of their p. 12.
  - The ledger is that of a West End bank lending to "noblemen, officers, church officials and wealthy traders" (p. 746). That population cannot test insurance for the poor.
- **The methodological lesson** is about the unit of observation. Borrower welfare is inferred from a lender's loan book. Borrowers who were dropped simply vanish from the data, and "access to credit" is treated as good in itself.
  - The bank's own survival is taken seriously; the borrowers' is not.
  - The sample is conditioned on survival: Hoare's is the one bank whose archive survived, and the authors concede "we know little about the many banks … that did not survive" (p. 756).
  - Both Glaeser–Scheinkman and Temin–Voth reason over ensembles. Neither looks at a trajectory.
- **Question it leaves:** if the barrier is the real issue, has anyone put it into a model?

### Step 3. The barrier enters the model

Reed and Bekar 2003, "Religious Prohibitions against Usury." Read pp. 347–358. Bekar 2001 is the working-paper precursor.

- **What to look for:**
  - The agents are **risk-neutral**, and an agent whose income falls below subsistence starves (p. 357).
  - The Church's "foremost goal is to keep all members of the population alive" (p. 357).
  - Pooling increases "community survivability by transferring income from those above subsistence to those below" (p. 353, n. 27).
  - The ban stops the rich from leaving the informal pool for the capital market, a defection that "threatens the survivability of those remaining in the pool" (p. 356).
- **How close it comes to the time-average view:**
  - The value function is a sum over the agent's remaining life, in which the per-period probability of staying alive multiplies across periods (p. 357). Absorption truncates the path, and the agent values the path, not one period.
  - The Church's preferences are lexicographic: survival comes first and revenue second (p. 357). That is the institutional form of safety-first.
  - The empirical section is a trajectory argument. Between 1300 and 1347 every spell of "starvation" wages lasted longer than a year, so a consumption loan "could not have been repaid before the next period of starvation wages began" (p. 355).
- **Where it stops short:**
  - In the model, debt is harmless. Lenders are risk-neutral, information is complete, and loans "leave the present value of the lender's net wealth unchanged". A rich borrower who lets debt accumulate up to the present value of his future income "is assured of never starving" (p. 359). The poor simply cannot borrow.
  - The danger of credit is therefore to the *pool*, not to the borrower. The rich leave for the capital market, and the poor who depend on their transfers starve.
  - The ban works through belief: it lowers the probability of salvation for those who borrow at interest (p. 360). It does not work through the dynamics of debt.
  - The persistent shocks of p. 355 — the ones that would make accumulated debt itself absorbing — are documented in the text and left out of the model, which assumes independent shocks.
- **Why it matters:** this is position 2 at its strongest, and the paper a referee will cite against any claim that the survival reading is new. The opening for HistorEE is precisely what the model assumes away: debt dynamics under persistent or multiplicative shocks, where the fixed claim is itself the route to absorption.
- **Question it leaves:** the ban here protects an insurance pool from credit. Could insurance itself be the problem?

### Step 4. Insurance creates the problem the ban solves

Rubin 2009, "Social Insurance, Commitment, and the Origin of Law." Read pp. 761–774.

- **What to look for:**
  - The puzzle is timing. There was no Christian ban on interest before the fourth century, when the Church first became rich enough to insure (p. 762). The insurance reading predicts the opposite order.
  - The model has an explicit exit at subsistence, and an insurer that maximises "the number of agents in the economy" (p. 766).
  - A rich Church cannot credibly refuse to bail out those who overborrow. Agents therefore overborrow, and the ban curbs it: "The ban is effective because it limits (over-)borrowing, not because it helps smooth consumption" (p. 766).
- **Why it matters:** this is the project's skin-in-the-game argument, in a model, about a doctrine. The insurer has relocated the barrier from the borrower's path onto itself, and the ban restores the borrower's exposure.
- **Then read Rubin 2011** (*Economic Journal*). It explains why the ban persisted in Islam and eroded in Western Europe: in Islam political authority depended more on religious legitimacy. Origin and persistence get different explanations, from the same author.
- **Question it leaves:** these models have a barrier but no compounding, and survival is a head count. Is there evidence that people *judged* rules by their effect on the path rather than the average?

### Step 5. People judge claims by the chance of disaster

Scott 1976, *The Moral Economy of the Peasant*. Read the introduction and chapter 1, pp. 1–34. Then read Kunreuther and Wright 1974.

- **What to look for in Scott:**
  - The cultivator seeks to "minimize the probability of having a disaster rather than maximizing his average return" (p. 18). The term "safety-first" comes from Roumasset.
  - Scattered strips are the first safety-first technique, "often at the cost of a reduction in average" yield (p. 5). Peters and Adamou list land scattering as cooperation that raises time-average growth: one practice, a loss on the average and a gain on the path.
  - The central passage is on pp. 29–31. The peasant's criterion is "what is left after outside claims have been met." The test of any exaction is "whether they increase or reduce the chance of a disaster." A variable claim that steadies the household's residual takes more in total, so that by the average take it "would qualify as the most exploitative." Yet it is the one peasants resent least (p. 31).
- **Why it matters:** this is the split between average and trajectory, drawn by hand and observed as a moral judgement.
- **A caveat:** Scott himself uses the language of risk aversion (p. 4). The trajectory reading is ours.
- **What to look for in Kunreuther and Wright:** farmers below a minimum holding are "forced to gamble" on the high-variance crop. Near the barrier, the model predicts risk-taking, not caution. A globally concave utility function cannot produce this; a barrier model does. It also squares Scott with later findings that peasants are sometimes risk-seeking.
- **Question it leaves:** do *doctrines*, and not only sentiments, sort claims by whether they move with the outcome?

### Step 6. Doctrine sorts debts by whether repayment moves with the outcome

Hudson 2002, "Reconstructing the Origins of Interest-Bearing Debt and the Logic of Clean Slates." Read pp. 27–41.

- **What to look for:**
  - Mesopotamian rulers annulled "barley debts, while leaving commercial debts intact." The reason given is that "Most commercial debts could be paid without creating social strains" (p. 28).
  - Commercial debts were already forgiven "when the merchandise was lost through no fault of the merchant" (p. 29). The spared debts were the ones whose repayment already moved with the outcome. The cancelled ones were fixed claims, paid from labour or assets whatever the harvest.
  - The rationale is the polity's survival: debts must not "expropriate the community's fighting force," and unchecked foreclosure would end in "dooming debt-ridden realms to defeat" (pp. 29–30). Hammurapi §§26–39 protected soldiers' lands from forfeiture (p. 30).
  - Hudson sees the multiplicative side: debts "grow autonomously … by the exponential curves of compound interest" (p. 41). He does not draw the trajectory consequence.
- **Caution:** Hudson argues for debt jubilees. Use him together with the Assyriological literature, not alone.
- **Question it leaves:** does the Islamic material run the same way, and how has it been explained?

### Step 7. The Islamic counterpart, and a methodological fork

El-Gamal 2001 on *gharar* (working paper, May 2001; published in *Islamic Economic Studies*), with its companion paper on *ribā*.

- **What to look for:**
  - *Gharar* is "trading in risk", which is "generally inefficient relative to other forms of risk sharing" (p. 1). El-Gamal explicitly rejects the idea that the rule targets zero-sum exchange (pp. 2, 6–7).
  - His mechanism is behavioural (pp. 7, 15–19). Agents with prospect-theory preferences accept dominated risk-trading contracts, and the prohibition protects them from themselves.
  - The *ribā* paper explains the prohibition by "individual discounting anomalies."
- **Why it matters:** this is the fork the project must argue at. One explanation of a doctrine adds psychology to the expectation; the other changes the dynamics. Ergodicity economics derives apparently "anomalous" discounting from multiplicative dynamics. If that derivation holds, El-Gamal's *ribā* argument can be restated without assuming biased agents.
- **Question it leaves:** granted the mechanism, how would one test it?

### Step 8. How to test it: rules evaluated by survival

Aktipis et al. 2016 on Maasai need-based transfers, with Campennì, Cronk and Aktipis 2017. Then Sosis and Bressler 2003.

- **What to look for in Aktipis et al.:**
  - The Maasai *osotua* norm reads "ask only if you are in need … give if you are able to do so without threatening your own survival" (p. 353). The barrier is written into the moral rule itself.
  - In their agent-based model a herd below 64 head for two consecutive years is removed. The outcome is herd survival over decades.
  - Need-based transfers outlast account-keeping (debt-based) transfers.
  - The 2017 paper shows pooling failing when disasters are correlated across households, which is the limit any pooling argument must state.
- **What to look for in Sosis and Bressler:** they measure how long 83 nineteenth-century US communes lasted against the costliness of their rules. This is the one historical design in the set with survival as the dependent variable.
- **Question it leaves:** what rival explanations must a survival reading defeat?

### Step 9. The rivals and the warnings

- **Rival functions** (read the abstracts at least):
  - Seror 2018: the prohibition consolidates clerical control.
  - Koyama 2010/2011: the ban as a barrier to entry that earned monopoly rents.
  - Schein 2003: the biblical ban as a limit on creditor power.
  - Ekelund, Hébert and Tollison 1989: usury rules as Church rent-seeking.
- **Comparative:** Calder 2016 argues that interest bans live and die with the standing of religious jurists, across all three traditions. It is the best single comparative paper.
- **Warnings:** Whitehouse et al. 2022 and Turchin et al. 2022 find that social complexity often *preceded* moralising religion. Read them before claiming that any norm arose because of its survival value.

## What the sweep corrected

The order above rests on full-text reading. The abstracts misled in six cases, and the corrections are themselves a finding.

1. **"Nobody reads doctrine as ruin avoidance."** False. Reed and Bekar 2003 and Rubin 2009 both model exit at subsistence. What is missing is the compounding dynamic and a measured survival outcome.
2. **Rubin 2009.** From the abstract it reads as the ban supporting in-group insurance. In the text the ban curbs the moral hazard that insurance creates, and the paper argues *against* the insurance reading.
3. **Reed and Bekar 2003.** From the abstract it reads as a consumption-smoothing argument. In the model the agents are risk-neutral, and all the value of pooling comes from the barrier.
4. **El-Gamal 2001.** From the abstract it reads as an efficiency argument under expected utility. In the text it is behavioural (prospect theory) and paternalist.
5. **Temin and Voth 2008.** It is cited as the refutation of the insurance reading. It tests predictions Glaeser and Scheinkman did not make, and its main findings match their own p. 12.
6. **Richardson and McBride 2009.** It was listed as a study of guild longevity. Its "longevity" is the life expectancy of guild members; it models how high mortality and the doctrine of purgatory sustained cooperation in religious guilds.

## What is open for HistorEE

- **A multiplicative model of a doctrine.** Compare a fixed claim with an outcome-contingent claim on the time-average growth rate and the absorption probability of a household. Pin the parameters to a historical case.
- **A trajectory criterion.** Evaluate rules by what they do to a single exposed household's path over time, not by a cross-section of agents or a survival count in one bad year.
- **A survival outcome.** Measure absorptions among the population at risk, longitudinally: forfeitures, debt bondage, disappearance from the record. Do not measure the allocation of a lender's book.
- **The filter, kept apart from the origin.** Any argument that a doctrine persisted because of its survival value must beat the legitimacy, clerical-control and rent explanations, and must deal with survivorship in the sources themselves.

## Questions for discussion

1. Which historical case gives us both the doctrine and longitudinal data on households at risk? Candidates include Old Babylonian debt records, English manorial and bankruptcy records, Ottoman court registers and Geniza partnerships.
2. Should the formal contribution extend Reed and Bekar and Rubin by adding multiplicative dynamics, or start from Peters and Adamou and add the doctrine? The first puts us in the economic-history conversation; the second puts us in the ergodicity-economics one.
3. How do we handle Kunreuther and Wright's result that the barrier induces gambling at the margin? It is a prediction that separates our reading from risk aversion, and it is testable.
