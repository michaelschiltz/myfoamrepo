> Τὸ τῷ σμήνει μὴ συμφέρον οὐδὲ τῇ μελίσσῃ συμφέρει.
> What is not advantageous to the swarm is not advantageous to the bee either.
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 6.54

## Summary

1. **The hive has now carried three distinct loads in the project, and they should be kept apart.** *Meditations* 6.54 states an asymmetric part–whole relation; the comb (Hales 2001) shows an optimum that exists only among neighbours and is the cheap container of a shared store; the colony as an *immunological individual* shows something neither of the first two can: a whole that draws its own boundary, polices its own members and defends itself as a unit. Only the third is emergence in the strong sense.

2. **Social immunity is the right image of emergence, but for a more precise reason than "the colony fights disease collectively".** The founding literature (Cremer, Armitage and Schmid-Hempel 2007; Cremer and Sixt 2009; Cremer, Pull and Fürst 2018) reserves social immunity in the strict sense for colonies in which the unit of selection has already shifted from the worker to the colony through a germline–soma division. Social immunity is therefore a *consequence* of the transition, not merely evidence for it. Pull and McMahon (2020) go further and treat superorganism immunity as itself a major transition in immune-system evolution, whose functions include defining and policing the colony's "self".

3. **The claim that game theory "does not conceive of the emergence of the whole or the rewriting of the rules" is too strong as it stands and would be contested by a referee.** A substantial body of work since about 2007 endogenises the game: evolving payoff matrices, game–environment feedback, game transitions, social niche construction, self-assembling games, and in institutional economics Greif and Laitin's endogenous institutional change.

4. **What survives, and is sharper, is this: those formalisms let the payoffs, the environment and the interaction structure change, but not the identity of the player.** The bearer of the payoff remains the lower-level individual throughout. When a model does let a higher-level unit emerge (Clejan, Congleton and Lerch 2022), individual fitness ceases to be well defined: the formalism registers emergence as the point at which its own accounting breaks down. Okasha (2022) gives the canonical formulation: in transition theory the hierarchy moves from the explanans into the explanandum. In the vocabulary of rules, the distinction is between *regulative* rules (which games can endogenise) and *constitutive* rules (which, Hédoin 2015 argues, game theory can accommodate only by abandoning methodological individualism).

5. **Three formal routes to "more than the sum of its parts" share a single root: non-additivity.** Multiplicative compounding (Peters and Adamou 2022: pooling raises time-average growth by σ²/2·(1−1/N)), convex fitness trade-offs (Michod 2006: group fitness equals mean cell fitness minus a negative covariance) and resource entanglement (Clejan et al. 2022). Standard payoffs are additive and history-independent; Fant et al. (2023) make exactly this point against the public-goods framing. Additivity excludes emergence by construction.

6. **Ergodicity dissolves the cooperation dilemma but does not, on its own, produce a new individual.** Peters and Adamou model existing entities that pool and share; they do not model the formation of a new unit, and say so in effect. The hive marks the further step: division of labour, policing and a defended boundary. HistorEE's pooling contracts sit mostly on the near side of that step; entity-shielded, perpetual forms approach the far side.

7. **A proposal to put to Peters and Connaughton.** Bourrat et al. (2022) show that cell- and collective-level fitness cannot decouple when measured over the same timescale: both converge on the same asymptotic growth rate. Read through ergodicity, decoupling is a short-horizon artefact and the levels coincide on the time-average horizon. That is a formal counterpart of 6.54's asymmetry, and it connects directly to WP3's spatial-versus-temporal ergodicity distinction.

8. **The metaphor has a rival lineage that the proposal should know it is choosing against.** Mandeville's hive (1714) is the hive of order emerging from self-interested parts under fixed rules: it is, in effect, the game-theoretic hive, and Hayek claimed it as an ancestor of spontaneous order. The Stoic hive is the other one.

## 1. Scope and method

The sweep was run in a dedicated Undermind workspace, *HistorEE — the hive: emergence, social immunity, rewriting the game*, with five deep searches: (i) social immunity and colony-level defence; (ii) superorganism, organismality and the major transitions in individuality; (iii) games that rewrite their own rules; (iv) cooperation and pooling under multiplicative growth; (v) the beehive as a metaphor in political, economic and scientific thought. Together they returned 1,059 ranked candidates. About twenty central texts were read in full through Undermind's PDF reader; the rest are characterised from abstracts and metadata, and are marked as such where it matters. Four important items were not available in full text and are treated on the abstract only: Boomsma and Gawne (2018), Worden and Levin (2007), Akçay and Roughgarden (2011) and Akçay (2020). Monographs (Maynard Smith and Szathmáry 1995; Buss 1987; Hölldobler and Wilson 2009; Okasha 2006) are cited for positions that are standard in the literature, not from a fresh reading.

## 2. Three hives, not one

The image entered the proposal as an epigraph and has since accumulated meanings that do not all point the same way.

*The Stoic hive.* *Meditations* 6.54 is asymmetric: the swarm's advantage is a necessary, not a sufficient, condition for the bee's. It asserts a constraint on the part from the whole, not an identity of interests. That asymmetry is what makes it serviceable for an argument about loss-sharing, where lopsided contracts concentrate variance on one party and endanger the survival of the pool.

*The geometric hive.* The honeycomb theorem (Hales 2001) shows that the regular hexagon minimises wall per unit area only for cells that share walls; a solitary cell would be round. The earlier session concluded that the comb is the cheap container of a risk pool, and that the expensive evolutionary achievement is not the hexagon but the association that makes shared walls possible. The geometry is static and additive; it shows cost-sharing, not the dynamics of what is pooled.

*The immunological hive.* The colony that detects infection, raises its own temperature, removes infected brood and distinguishes nestmates from intruders is a third image. Here the whole does something none of its parts does, and does it *to* its parts: it sacrifices some to preserve the rest, and it defines who belongs. This is the hive that bears on emergence in the strong sense, and it is the one the rest of this report follows.

## 3. The colony as an immunological individual

### 3.1 The concept

Cremer, Armitage and Schmid-Hempel (2007) introduced *social immunity* for the collective anti-parasite defences of insect societies, by explicit analogy with the immune system of a multicellular body. The 2018 review by Cremer, Pull and Fürst defines it as protection arising from the collectively performed behavioural, physiological and organisational defences of colony members, and calls it "the immune system of the colony" (Cremer, Pull and Fürst 2018, 107). The repertoire is now well documented in honey bees and ants:

- *hygienic behaviour*: detection and removal of diseased brood, heritable and selectable in honey bees, with demonstrated effects on American foulbrood (Spivak and Reuter 2001);
- *social fever*: workers raise brood-nest temperature in response to a heat-sensitive fungal pathogen (Starks, Blackie and Seeley 2000);
- *antimicrobial nest materials*: resin collection, which increases after parasite challenge (Simone, Evans and Spivak 2009);
- *social vaccination*: low-level transfer of a fungal pathogen during grooming induces protective immune responses in nestmates (Konrad et al. 2012);
- *organisational immunity*: ant colonies reorganise their contact networks after pathogen exposure in ways that reduce transmission (Stroeymeyt et al. 2018);
- *social apoptosis*: in *Apis cerana*, mite-infested brood dies early and is removed, interrupting parasite reproduction at the cost of the individual (Page et al. 2016).

### 3.2 Immunity presupposes the shift in the unit of selection

The analytically decisive point is easy to miss. Cremer, Pull and Fürst (2018, 107–8) do not treat every group defence as social immunity. Families have parental care; communal breeders have additive benefits of aggregation. Social immunity *in the strict sense* arises, on their account, only where selection has shifted from the individual to the colony through a germline–soma division: queens and males as germline, sterile workers as soma. Unconditional altruism in disease defence is expected in obligately eusocial superorganisms; facultatively social species should show only context-dependent defence, because members who can still reproduce retain conflicting interests.

The order of explanation therefore runs from the transition to the immune function, not the other way round. Cremer and Sixt (2009, 131) state the premise plainly: the colony as a whole, not any single individual, produces offspring. The immune analogy holds because there is now a reproductive unit to defend.

Pull and McMahon (2020) push this a step further. They argue that the colony-level defence system is itself a major transition in immune-system evolution: once a colony becomes a new individual it acquires "a bespoke defense system that operates at the level of the new individual" (Pull and McMahon 2020, 2). Its functions include not only anti-pathogen defence but the policing of internal cheaters (worker-laid eggs removed) and the maintenance of colony identity through colony-specific cuticular hydrocarbons — what they describe as setting the limits and boundaries of the organism. Pre-existing individual defences are co-opted and elaborated; the system is distributed and self-organised, with no central controller. McMahon (2017) develops the same argument for termites from the cockroach-like ancestor onward (abstract only).

For HistorEE this is the most useful formulation in the whole literature: the immune system of an emergent individual is the apparatus by which it *draws and defends its own boundary* — against external parasites, against internal defectors and against fusion with other units.

### 3.3 Division of labour between individual and collective defence

If the colony defends itself, selection on individual immunity may relax. The evidence is suggestive but not uniform:

- In the ant *Lasius niger*, individual systemic immunity responds to bacteria but not to fungal infection, whereas grooming and group living protect against fungi but not bacteria. The authors read this as complementary, non-overlapping defence and propose, without having tested it, that social care relaxed selection on individual antifungal immunity (Masson et al. 2024).
- Comparative work finds reduced cellular immune responses in social insect lineages (López-Uribe et al. 2016, abstract only) and reduced immune-gene diversity along the termite lineage (He et al. 2021).
- But the reduced immune repertoire of bees appears to *precede* sociality (Barribeau et al. 2015), so it cannot simply be a consequence of colony-level defence.

The general trade-off is a hypothesis with some support, not an established law. That matters if the report's analogy is carried into institutions: the claim that collective protection allows individual protection to atrophy should be stated as a possibility, not as a finding.

### 3.4 Genetic diversity as a property no individual has

Honey bee queens mate with many males. Colonies with higher genetic diversity suffer fewer severe infections and grow better (Tarpy 2003); colonies headed by queens experimentally inseminated by many drones show lower disease intensity than those headed by queens inseminated by one (Seeley and Tarpy 2007). Oldroyd and Fewell (2007) generalise the point: genetic diversity promotes colony homeostasis. The argument is a portfolio argument. No worker is diverse; diversity exists only at the level of the colony, and its effect, as Tarpy's title has it, is to prevent *severe* infections: to cut the lower tail rather than to raise the mean. That is the ruin-avoidance logic of the project in entomological form.

The caution is that a meta-analysis finds direct evidence for the disease-resistance benefit of polyandry only in eusocial Hymenoptera, with small samples and publication bias (Soper, Ekroth and Martins 2021). The honey-bee case is solid; its generalisation is not.

### 3.5 The status of the analogy

Cremer and Sixt (2009, 129) are explicit that they "only report phenomenological analogies in defence strategies", and make no claim that the mechanisms are related. Their evolutionary claim is that similar parasite pressures acting on similar reproductive units produced parallel solutions. They also list disanalogies: workers are genetically more diverse than somatic cells, so conflict is greater; a colony can expel members or abandon a nest, which a body cannot; and there is no evidence of social autoimmunity. The project should adopt the same discipline. The colony–organism analogy is a functional homoplasy, not a homology, which is precisely the kind of evidence the project already treats as informative (independent origin versus transmission).

## 4. The emergent level: superorganism and major transitions

### 4.1 From analogy to theory

Wheeler (1911) argued that the ant colony is an organism; the idea fell out of favour with the gene's-eye view and was revived by Wilson and Sober (1989) under multilevel selection. Seeley (1997) made the empirical case for the honey bee colony as a group-level adaptive unit, on the evidence of colony-level foraging, communication and feedback control; Moritz and Southwick (1992) and Hölldobler and Wilson (2009) consolidated the superorganism as a working concept.

The general theory came from the study of the major transitions: genes into genomes, prokaryotes into eukaryotes, cells into multicellular organisms, organisms into colonies (Buss 1987; Maynard Smith and Szathmáry 1995; Szathmáry 2015). The common structure is that entities which could once replicate independently come to replicate only as parts of a larger whole. Michod's programme (Michod 1999; Michod and Nedelcu 2003; Michod 2006) describes this as a reorganisation of fitness: cooperation creates fitness at the higher level, and conflict mediation (germ–soma separation, policing, developmental bottlenecks) protects it.

### 4.2 What counts as a transition

There is a live dispute over criteria, and it bears on where the honey bee sits:

- West et al. (2015) define a transition by *mutual dependence* plus *negligible within-group conflict*, and list the honey bee among completed transitions. They insist that a transition is "not driven simply by satisfying Hamilton's rule" (West et al. 2015, 10118): cooperation is necessary but not sufficient; integration must reach the point at which the group acts as a single maximising agent.
- Boomsma and Gawne (2018, abstract only) set a stricter criterion: permanently differentiated reproductive and worker castes, arising only under strict lifetime monogamy, mark a point of no return. Honey bees, with their highly polyandrous queens, are superorganismal on this account because caste differentiation became irreversible *before* polyandry evolved.
- Queller and Strassmann (2009) define organismality by high cooperation and very low conflict among parts, on a continuum.

The upshot for the project is that "the hive as an emergent individual" is not controversial for the honey bee, whatever criterion is chosen. What is controversial is how many *other* cooperative arrangements qualify, which is the right worry for an historian deciding which institutions are pools and which are persons.

### 4.3 Okasha's formulation

Okasha (2022) supplies the sentence the project's argument needs. In the traditional levels-of-selection debate the biological hierarchy is taken as given; in transition theory it becomes something to be explained. The hierarchy, in his words, "moves from being part of the explanans to being part of the explanandum" (Okasha 2022, 6). He adds that properties often written into the definition of an evolutionary unit, such as copying fidelity and organismic cohesion, are themselves evolved. He does not frame this as a bootstrapping paradox, and he does not claim that game-theoretic or kin-selection models all presuppose fixed units; he notes that multilevel analysis presupposes group structure while inclusive-fitness analysis does not. But the core point stands: an account of emergence must explain the level, not merely operate on it.

### 4.4 "More than the sum" in formal terms

Two results make the phrase precise rather than rhetorical.

Michod (2006) defines group fitness as the product of the group's mean viability and mean fecundity, *W = V·B*, and shows that it equals mean cell fitness minus the covariance of viability and fecundity across cells: *W = mean(w) − Cov(v, b)*. Where cells specialise along a convex trade-off, the covariance is negative, so group fitness exceeds the average fitness of its members. Specialisation costs each cell and benefits the group; germ and soma are, in this sense, altruists.

Clejan, Congleton and Lerch (2022, abstract only) show, within evolutionary game theory itself, that two constraints suffice for a well-defined group fitness to emerge: members contribute resources to one another in proportion to the producers' density, and invading groups do not share in those resources. The outcomes of invading individuals then become "entangled", so that *individual fitness can no longer be defined* and group fitness governs the dynamics. They describe group fitness as irreducible to the fitness of the parts and as exerting downward causation on them.

### 4.5 The sceptical counterweight

Two lines of work should temper the strong reading.

First, Bourrat et al. (2022) challenge the common claim that during a transition fitness is "decoupled" or "exported" from cells to collectives. In their model, once particle and collective fitness are measured over the same timescale and the same set of events, both grow at the rate set by the same dominant eigenvalue: they cannot diverge. Apparent decoupling arises from comparing cell performance over a short window with collective performance over a full collective generation. They propose instead to describe transitions as *trade-off breaking*: the lineage escapes an ancestral constraint among traits, yielding traits that exist only in a collective context. (See also Bourrat 2015.)

Second, Black, Bourrat and Rainey (2020) show how ecology can *scaffold* collective-level Darwinian properties before collectives possess them intrinsically. Patchy resources and periodic dispersal give patches variation, differential reproduction and heredity; with slow dispersal, slower-growing cell types win over the long patch cycle even while losing within each patch. These patch-level properties would instantly disappear, they note, if the scaffold were removed (Black, Bourrat and Rainey 2020, 6). Endogenisation of the scaffold is a further evolutionary step.

Neither line denies emergence. Both locate it more carefully: in timescale and in the internalisation of what was first imposed from outside.

## 5. Does game theory conceive of the game being rewritten?

### 5.1 The literature that does

The claim that game theory treats the game as fixed describes the textbook, not the field. The following all endogenise some part of the game:

- *Evolving payoffs.* Worden and Levin (2007, abstract only) show how mutation can carry a population out of the prisoner's dilemma altogether. Akçay and Roughgarden (2011, abstract only) model players who alter one another's payoffs by providing incentives. Stewart and Plotkin (2014) let payoffs and strategies coevolve in iterated games and find that cooperation often collapses as individuals maximise its benefits: average payoff can fall even as the potential reward for mutual cooperation rises, and evolution may leave the prisoner's dilemma entirely.
- *Game–environment feedback.* Weitz et al. (2016) couple replicator dynamics to a resource whose state changes the payoffs, producing an oscillating tragedy of the commons. Tilman, Plotkin and Akçay (2020) generalise the framework: strategies alter the environment and the environment alters the payoff matrix, yielding bistability, limit cycles and tipping points whose existence depends on the separation of timescales. They describe the conventional assumption as treating the nature of the interaction as fixed in time.
- *Game transitions.* Su et al. (2019) let the current game and the players' actions determine the game played next; transitions can lower the threshold for cooperation below that of any single fixed game.
- *Social niche construction.* Powers, Penn and Watson (2011) argue that models assuming static population structure cannot explain how the structures that support cooperation arise, and model the concurrent evolution of group-size preference and social behaviour. Ryan, Powers and Watson (2016) generalise this as social niche construction: organisms are "both the subject and object of their own social evolution" (61). Traits that alter assortment, dispersal or policing move the *effective game* across the payoff plane, from the prisoner's dilemma towards the harmony game; they argue that this can carry a lineage into a transition in individuality.
- *Programme statements.* Akçay (2020, abstract only) calls for the next stage of social evolution theory to explain the evolution of the setting in which social behaviour evolves. Barrett and Skyrms (2017, abstract only) model games that assemble themselves.
- *Institutional economics.* Greif and Laitin (2004) argue that neither the self-enforcing equilibrium of game theory nor historical institutionalism alone explains institutional change, and introduce quasi-parameters and self-reinforcement to let institutions undermine or reinforce themselves endogenously. This is the most directly relevant item, since Greif is one of the four names the project's central proposition inverts.

A referee familiar with any of this would reject the unqualified claim.

### 5.2 What these models still cannot represent

The sharper claim survives, and it is stronger than the original. Three features recur across the literature in §5.1.

*First, the player set is fixed.* Every model above changes payoffs, environments or interaction structures, but the bearer of the payoff is the same lower-level individual from start to finish. Ryan, Powers and Watson describe social niche construction as movement across the payoff plane; the plane's axes are still the individual's payoffs. A transition in individuality is the appearance of a new bearer, and the one model that represents it within game theory does so at the price of losing individual fitness as a well-defined quantity (Clejan, Congleton and Lerch 2022). The formalism records emergence as its own breakdown.

*Second, the rules of rewriting are themselves fixed.* Su et al.'s transition rule, Tilman et al.'s environmental dynamics and Akçay and Roughgarden's incentive technology are exogenous. Endogenising the game creates a game over games whose structure is given. The regress can be pushed back, but each step re-fixes a higher-order rule.

*Third, standard payoffs are additive and history-independent.* Fant et al. (2023, 5) note that in standard game-theoretic payoffs gains "do not accumulate in a multiplicative fashion" and do not depend on the history of play. To recover a game from multiplicative growth with sharing, they must treat the whole growth-and-sharing history as a *single* iteration, with long-run growth as the payoff. In other words, the game exists only at the level of the trajectory, not at the level of the interaction.

### 5.3 Regulative and constitutive rules

The phrase "rewriting the rules of the game, and arguably the game itself" maps onto a distinction philosophy of social science has drawn sharply. Regulative rules govern a pre-existing activity; constitutive rules create the activity and the positions within it (Searle's "X counts as Y in C"). Hédoin (2015, abstract only) asks whether game theory can account for constitutive rules and concludes that it can only on accounts that give up pure methodological individualism (rules as normative expectations or as correlating devices). The endogenous-game literature of §5.1 endogenises regulative rules. A transition in individuality is a change in constitutive rules: it creates a new position — *colony*, *partnership*, *corporation* — that counts as a bearer of interests in its own right.

This is the precise version of the break the proposal wants to mark, and it also places HistorEE correctly against North's definition of institutions as the rules of the game (North 1990): the project is interested in the moments at which a new kind of player is constituted, not only in changes to the rules players follow.

### 5.4 The strongest opposing case

West et al. (2015) and Gardner and Grafen (2009) hold that inclusive-fitness theory is adequate to the transitions. Gardner and Grafen formalise the superorganism as a group whose members behave as if maximising a common objective, which inclusive-fitness optimisation can capture when within-group conflict is negligible. On this view, nothing requires a new kind of theory; the group becomes a maximising agent when the conditions for alignment are met. The proposal should acknowledge this position. The reply available to it is that the alignment conditions are what require explanation, and that in a multiplicative world alignment can arise from the growth dynamics themselves rather than from relatedness (§6).

## 6. Where ergodicity enters, and where it stops

### 6.1 The baseline result

Peters and Adamou (2022) model entities whose resources follow geometric Brownian motion. Alone, each grows at the time-average rate μ − σ²/2. If *N* entities pool and share equally at each step, the effective volatility falls to σ/√N and each grows at μ − σ²/2N; the advantage, σ²/2·(1−1/N), comes from reduced volatility, not from higher mean drift, and requires neither relatedness nor reciprocity. Judged by the ensemble average there is no incentive to cooperate; judged by the time average there is. They conclude that "the interests of the group and its members are fully aligned" (Peters and Adamou 2022, 8). Yaari and Solomon (2010) reached a cognate result in random multiplicative environments, and Stojkoski et al. (2019) show that network structure and heterogeneity limit its generality.

Fant et al. (2023) add a result the proposal should use. With a finite time horizon there is a critical horizon *T\**: below it, defection is favoured; above it, positive sharing is stable, and at long horizons sharing approaches full cooperation. Correlated fluctuations reduce the benefit (the relevant fluctuation is σ²(1−ρ)). The time horizon is therefore an empirical variable, not a philosophical stance. For an historian, the length of the horizon over which a partnership, a *kō* or a waqf was expected to run becomes a testable determinant of how much it pooled.

### 6.2 The convergence with social-evolution theory, and its limits

The same structure has appeared independently in evolutionary biology. Kennedy et al. (2018) derive a stochastic Hamilton's rule in which helping can be favoured because it reduces the volatility of relatives' reproduction, even when it does not raise their mean. Kuosmanen, Minetto and Mustonen (2025) survey non-ergodicity in ecology and evolution, cite Peters, and show among other things that cooperators can decline within each patch while remaining common in the metapopulation, so that the global frequency differs from that of the typical patch. They do not, however, address transitions in individuality.

The empirical evidence is mixed. Dos Santos et al. (2024), from Stuart West's group, examined fifteen cooperatively breeding bird species and found that helping did not consistently reduce reproductive variance and that mean benefits generally dominated. Capilla-Lasheras et al. (2021) report the opposite in a Kalahari bird. The variance-reduction pathway is theoretically secure and empirically contested; the project should not cite it as settled biology.

### 6.3 The step ergodicity does not take

Peters and Adamou model existing entities that pool and share. They do not model the formation of a new entity: the total resources of cooperators are more than the sum of the parts as a result of the growth comparison, not because a new unit with its own boundary, division of labour and defences has come into being. They mention the transition to multicellularity as motivation, not as a result. The hive is exactly what lies beyond: the honey bee colony stores and shares honey (the pool), but it also separates germline from soma, polices worker reproduction, recognises nestmates and defends itself as a unit.

So the project has two levels to keep apart:

- **Pooling**: lower-level units remain the bearers of interest; pooling raises each one's time-average growth. Ergodicity explains why this is the expected case.
- **Individuation**: a new unit becomes the bearer of interest, with its own boundary and continuity. Ergodicity supplies the reason the units' interests align on the long horizon; it does not by itself explain the boundary, the policing or the perpetuity.

### 6.4 A proposal: decoupling as a short-horizon artefact

The following is an interpretation, not a finding of any paper read here, and it should be put to Peters and Connaughton before it is used.

Bourrat et al. (2022) show that cell and collective fitness converge on the same asymptotic growth rate (the dominant eigenvalue) when measured commensurably, and that apparent decoupling comes from comparing short-window cell performance with long-window collective performance. An asymptotic growth rate is a time-average quantity. Read through ergodicity, their result says that the levels can diverge only on short horizons and coincide on the horizon that counts. Black, Bourrat and Rainey (2020) give the mechanism a concrete form: slower-growing cells lose within each patch but win over the long patch cycle, which is the ensemble-versus-time divergence at the level of cells and patches.

If this holds, it gives 6.54 a formal counterpart. On the time-average horizon, what does not profit the swarm does not profit the bee; on shorter horizons the bee may profit at the swarm's expense, which is precisely the condition under which the colony needs policing and an immune boundary. The asymmetry of the Stoic sentence would then be the asymmetry between horizons. This connects to WP3's distinction between spatial ergodicity (Kelly-style spatial noise) and temporal ergodicity (space-for-time substitution), and it is worth a short note for the theorists.

### 6.5 Non-additivity as the common root

The three formal routes to "more than the sum" identified above share one feature:

| Route | Source | Non-linearity |
|---|---|---|
| Pooling under multiplicative growth | Peters and Adamou 2022; Fant et al. 2023 | Compounding: the time average is not the ensemble average (Jensen) |
| Germ–soma specialisation | Michod 2006 | Convex trade-off: W = mean(w) − Cov(v, b) |
| Emergent group fitness | Clejan, Congleton and Lerch 2022 | Resource entanglement: individual fitness undefined |

Where payoffs add, the whole is the sum of its parts by construction. The break with game theory is therefore not a matter of temperament but of algebra: emergence requires an aggregation rule that is not additive, and the standard payoff matrix is additive and memoryless. Stated this way the claim is defensible against a hostile referee.

## 7. Implications for the institutional argument

### 7.1 Legal scaffolding

Black, Bourrat and Rainey's concept transfers to institutions with little strain. Many historical pools had collective properties that were *imposed from outside* — by a court, a guild, a temple, a notarial formula, a lord's charter — and that would have vanished if the external support were withdrawn. The decomposition strand already treats the corporation as a bundle of separable components (entity shielding, capital lock-in, transferable claims, legal personality, perpetual succession) available long before their combination. The scaffolding vocabulary gives a dynamic reading of the same observation: the corporation is the form in which the scaffold has been *internalised*, so that the collective's boundary and continuity no longer depend on an outside party. That is the institutional analogue of a completed transition. It would also explain why recombination, not invention, is the historically interesting event.

### 7.2 The immune functions of institutions

Read with Cremer and Sixt's discipline (phenomenological analogy, not mechanism), the colony's three immune tasks have clear institutional counterparts:

- *Border defence* corresponds to **entity shielding**: protecting the pool's assets from the personal creditors of its members (Hansmann, Kraakman and Squire 2006), so that one member's ruin does not infect the whole.
- *Policing internal cheaters* corresponds to doctrines that police **lopsided contracts** and opportunistic withdrawal, the loss-sharing rules the project already studies (*al-ghurm bi-l-ghunm*, the prohibition of the *societas leonina*).
- *Self/non-self recognition* corresponds to **membership rules**: who may join, who may be expelled, whether a pool may merge with another.

The point is not that institutions are organisms. It is that where a pool has crossed into individuation, one should expect to find these three functions, and where they are absent, one is dealing with a pool rather than a person. That is a coding criterion, not a metaphor.

### 7.3 The diversity argument

The polyandry results (§3.4) supply a precise biological parallel for the claim that institutions survive by diversifying exposure rather than by maximising expected return. They also carry the same caution: the effect is robust in honey bees and poorly supported elsewhere. The parallel is illustrative, not evidential.

## 8. The metaphor's own history

The hive has been claimed by both sides of the argument this report describes, and the proposal should know which hive it is quoting.

The classical hive, from Aristotle and Virgil's fourth *Georgic* to Seneca's *De clementia*, was a ruler-led polity in which the whole had priority (Van Overmeire 2012; Hardie 2020; Knight 2021, all on abstracts). Marcus Aurelius belongs to this lineage, although his asymmetric formulation is more careful than most. Early modern natural history unsettled the monarchical reading when the "king" bee was found to be a queen (Merrick 2021; Crachiolo 2018).

Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees* (1714) inverted the classical hive: public prosperity arises from private vice, and the attempt to make the hive honest ruins it. Kapust and Turner read this as a deliberate subversion of the classical apiary. Hayek claimed Mandeville as an ancestor of spontaneous order; Nolan (2016) traces the route through Sakmann and Schatz, and Dale (2026) offers a critical countergenealogy that brings out the class and racial hierarchies in both writers' appeals to animal nature. Mandeville's hive is, structurally, the game-theoretic hive: order emerges from self-interested parts acting under fixed rules, and no whole is presupposed.

Darwin used the graded series of bee cells to show that the honey bee's comb could have been reached gradually; Marx contrasted the worst architect with the best bee, on the ground that the architect raises the structure in imagination before building it (*Capital*, vol. 1, ch. 7). Maeterlinck's "spirit of the hive" and Wheeler's superorganism gave the collective whole a twentieth-century vocabulary with political resonances that historians of science have treated warily. Rodgers (2008) shows how entomology and sociology borrowed from each other in ways that naturalised the social hierarchies of the day.

Two consequences follow. First, the project's epigraph is not neutral; it takes a side against Mandeville. Ergodicity gives that side a new and stronger argument: the bee's interest and the swarm's coincide on the long horizon without requiring either the Stoic subordination of the part or Mandeville's alchemy of vice. Second, Rodgers's warning and Cremer and Sixt's insistence on "phenomenological" analogy are the same discipline in two disciplines. The proposal should use the hive as an image of a formal structure, and say so.

## 9. Recommendations

1. **Rewrite the game-theory claim.** *Recommendation: replace any statement that game theory cannot conceive of changing rules with the narrower and stronger claim that fixed-player formalisms cannot represent the emergence of a new bearer of interests.* Suggested wording: "Game-theoretic accounts can let payoffs and interaction structures evolve, but they hold the identity of the player fixed; the historical question is how new kinds of player — the partnership, the endowment, the corporation — were constituted. The hierarchy belongs in the explanandum." Cite Okasha (2022), Tilman, Plotkin and Akçay (2020) and Greif and Laitin (2004).

2. **Use social immunity once, for the boundary, not as evidence.** *Recommendation: if the hive appears beyond the epigraph, use the immunological image to make a single point — that an emergent unit draws and defends its own boundary — with Pull and McMahon (2020) as the citation.* Do not use it as evidence for any claim about institutions.

3. **Put the horizon reading of Bourrat to the theorists.** *Recommendation: draft a one-page note for Peters and Connaughton on §6.4 (decoupling as a short-horizon artefact) and its relation to the spatial/temporal ergodicity distinction in WP3, before anything goes into the proposal.*

4. **Adopt "legal scaffolding" provisionally for the decomposition strand.** *Recommendation: test the concept against two or three coded cases (one pooling contract, one waqf, one entity-shielded form) in HistorEE_codebooks before using it in the text; if it holds, it gives the five-component decomposition a dynamic interpretation.*

5. **Add the three immune functions as a coding probe.** *Recommendation: add border defence (entity shielding), internal policing (anti-lopsided-contract doctrine) and membership control as a composite "individuation" indicator in the codebook schema, distinguishing pools from persons.*

6. **Keep Mandeville in view.** *Recommendation: one sentence in Part 1, at most, acknowledging that the hive has served the rival tradition; Dale (2026) is the reading to have done before writing it.*

7. **Cite the time-horizon result.** *Recommendation: cite Fant et al. (2023) wherever the proposal states that cooperation is the expected case; the critical-horizon result makes the claim empirically testable in historical material.*
